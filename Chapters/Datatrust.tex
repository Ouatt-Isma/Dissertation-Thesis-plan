\chapter{Assessment of AI Training Datasets Trustworthiness}
\label{chap:dataset-trust}
The previous chapter presented the overall architecture of \ac{PaTAS}, highlighting its two main components: (i) the Trust Assessment Module focusing on the assessment of the trustworthiness of the training dataset and the input of the \ac{NN}, and (ii) the Trust Propagation Module focusing on the propagation of trust through the model, from the training data to the learned parameters and from the input to the output via these parameters. This chapter focuses on the first component, namely the trust assessment module. The underlying ideas were initially explored in the paper presented at the BIAS Workshop at ECML PKDD~\cite{10.1007/978-3-032-19096-3_17} and further developed in the paper on labeling accuracy presented at the TRUST-AI Workshop of ECAI~\cite{trust-aidatatrust}. In this dissertation, these contributions are unified and extended into a comprehensive top-level framework for assessing the trustworthiness of training datasets and, by extension, of the input data of a \aclu{NN} at inference time.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:intro}

The chapter is organized around three observations. First, \emph{the trustworthiness of a training dataset is not a scalar.} Different trustworthiness properties such as fairness, correctness, representativeness, privacy compliance, and robustness to leakage require different types of evidence, different evaluation granularities, and different aggregation rules. Collapsing them into a single quality score, or applying the same metric, the same threshold, and the same aggregation rule across all of them, hides exactly the information that should drive downstream decisions: which property is at risk, at which stage of the lifecycle.

Second, \emph{properties live at different levels of the data}. Some properties are only meaningful at the \textbf{dataset level} (e.g., class balance, coverage). Others are intrinsically \textbf{per-entry} (e.g., label correctness) or \textbf{per-feature} (e.g., sensor reliability). The same scheme used at inference time on individual inputs can be applied for entry- and feature-level dataset properties, which is what makes input-data trust assessment a special case of dataset trust assessment rather than a separate problem.

Third, \emph{evidence about trustworthiness is rarely complete}. Training datasets are typically constructed through multi-stage lifecycles in which documentation, metadata, and direct observations are partial, sometimes contradictory, and often unavailable for historical pipelines. The framework must therefore distinguish \emph{low support} from \emph{no support}, which is exactly what \aclu{SL} offers and what scalar quality scores cannot.

Building on these observations, the chapter introduces a top-level framework for assessing the trustworthiness of AI training datasets. The framework is a direct specialization of the General Trust Assessment Methodology of \cref{chap:Trust_method} to the dataset setting, and provides the conceptual and formal foundation on which several specific contributions of this thesis build: the assessment of sampling and representation bias~\cite{10.1007/978-3-032-19096-3_17}, the assessment of labeling accuracy from annotator disagreement~\cite{trust-aidatatrust}, and the multi-stage analysis illustrated on \ac{COMPAS} dataset. 
The remainder of the chapter is organized as follows. \cref{sec:dataset-trust:uncertainty} reviews the sources of uncertainty that arise at different stages of the dataset lifecycle and motivates the use of an evidence-based formalism. \cref{sec:dataset-trust:framework} introduces the three design principles of the framework, organizes them into a three-axis design space, and re-states the three phases of the General Methodology in dataset-specific language. \cref{sec:dataset-trust:applications:compas} provides the metric-to-evidence mapping application on the COMPAS Dataset. \cref{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb,sec:dataset-trust:applications:cifar10h} demonstrate the framework on two other applications spanning different cells of the design space. \cref{sec:dataset-trust:summary} concludes.

\section{Sources of Uncertainty in Training Datasets Assessment}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:uncertainty}

Uncertainty is an inherent component of any assessment process, including but not limited to the evaluation of AI training datasets. More fundamentally, uncertainty does not arise from a single cause but from multiple, qualitatively different mechanisms. These mechanisms differ in whether they can be adequately modeled using classical probability theory or whether they require an evidence-based uncertainty framework such as \aclu{SL}.

\subsection{Fundamental Sources of Uncertainty}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:uncertainty:sources}

Across domains, uncertainty in trust assessment typically originates from the
following sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Randomness in nature}, reflecting inherent variability in the variable (proposition to assess); e.g., repeated evaluations of the same process yielding different outcomes due to intrinsic variability, leading to uncertainty about whether the assessed system consistently satisfies a reliability property;
  % \item \textbf{Noise in the evidence}, arising from measurement errors, annotation mistakes, or stochastic perturbations; e.g., imperfect or noisy observations providing fluctuating support for a trust proposition, such as inconsistent measurements or annotations affecting confidence in the assessment;
  \item \textbf{System complexity}, where processes or interactions between components are only partially observable or poorly understood; e.g., uncertainty in trust arising from the interaction of multiple subsystems or lifecycle stages, where the contribution of individual components to the overall reliability cannot be cleanly isolated;
  \item \textbf{Conflicting evidence}, where different diagnostics or information sources support incompatible conclusions; e.g., one assessment indicating that a trust requirement is satisfied while another, based on a different perspective or metric, suggests the opposite;
  \item \textbf{Lack of evidence}, where relevant information is missing, incomplete, or unavailable; e.g., insufficient documentation or unavailable audit results preventing a confident determination of whether a trust property holds.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Addressable by Classical Probability Theory}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:uncertainty:probability}
While these sources often co-occur in practice, they differ significantly in how they should be modeled and interpreted. Classical probability theory is well suited for modeling uncertainty that arises from inherent randomness and stochastic noise, commonly referred to as aleatoric uncertainty. In this setting, uncertainty reflects variability in outcomes rather than a lack of knowledge.

In the assessment of training datasets, such uncertainty may arise when the trust source itself is governed by a stochastic process: stochastic measurement noise (e.g., sensor precision limits) affecting the values a diagnostic returns or natural variability in observed phenomena making the same metric fluctuate across draws. For example, a sensor-consistency score computed on repeated measurements of the same physical quantity may vary slightly due to sensor noise. When the process generating the evidence is well specified and sufficient observations are available, probability distributions can be estimated and used to characterize the expected variability of the evidence, and therefore of the trust assessment that uses it.

However, a probabilistic framework implicitly assumes that (i) the process generating the evidence carries no uncertainty itself, (ii) sufficient evidence can be collected for residual uncertainty to be safely treated as zero, and (iii) the available evidence is never contradictory and incomplete at the same time. These assumptions are frequently violated in real-world dataset construction, where evidence about a property is typically partial, collected by heterogeneous trust sources, and sometimes contradictory.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Requiring Evidence-Based Modeling}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:uncertainty:evidence}

The remaining sources of uncertainty (system complexity, conflicting evidence, and lack of evidence) cannot be adequately represented using classical probability theory alone. These sources give rise to epistemic uncertainty, reflecting incomplete or unreliable knowledge about the dataset and its provenance.

\paragraph{System complexity.}
A dataset typically results from multiple interacting processes, such as sampling strategies, collection protocols, annotation workflows, and preprocessing pipelines. For instance, a dataset may combine data collected across different time periods, devices, or geographic regions, each with distinct characteristics. System complexity is not itself a source of uncertainty in the way that randomness or measurement noise are. It is an epistemic condition: when the contributing processes interact in ways the analyst cannot fully observe or model, the analyst's knowledge of whether the trust proposition holds is incomplete, and that incompleteness shows up as uncertainty in the trust assessment. Moreover, \ac{SL} can be used to reason in the context of complex systems through the graph based reasoning. 

\paragraph{Conflicting and incomplete evidence.}
Two further sources of uncertainty fall outside what classical probability can adequately represent. Conflicting evidence occurs when different trust sources provide incompatible signals: statistical metrics may indicate balanced group representation while an audit reveals undocumented exclusion criteria, or high inter-annotator agreement may coexist with label bias affecting a minority group. Lack of evidence arises when key information about the dataset lifecycle is missing or inaccessible, such as absent documentation of preprocessing steps or unavailable annotation guidelines. In both cases, collapsing all available signals into a single probability either obscures the underlying disagreement or silently treats absence of evidence as evidence of absence. \aclu{SL} addresses both failure modes by maintaining trust mass, distrust mass, and uncertainty as distinct quantities.

These sources of uncertainty do not arise in isolation in practice. AI training datasets are assembled through complex, multi-stage lifecycles involving heterogeneous actors, tools, and governance regimes, making the simultaneous presence of randomness, system complexity, conflicting signals, and missing information the norm rather than the exception. For this reason, the framework presented in the next section requires a formalism that accommodates both probabilistic variability and epistemic limitations. \aclu{SL} provides that foundation, though on its own it does not prescribe what propositions to formulate, which trust sources to consult, or how to combine them. Those choices are the role of the framework introduced next.

\section{A Framework for Trust Assessment of Training Datasets}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:framework}

This section presents the high-level structure of the proposed framework. The framework supports transparent and uncertainty-aware reasoning by integrating heterogeneous evidence across the dataset lifecycle. Rather than producing a single score, dataset trustworthiness is represented as a structured and property-dependent trust assessment that explicitly accounts for incomplete, conflicting, or missing information.
\label{sec:dataset-trust:framework:principles}
The framework is built on the following design principles.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Property-dependent assessment.} Trustworthiness is always defined with respect to a specific property of interest. The same dataset may be highly trustworthy for one property and not for another, in line with the trust-proposition formalism introduced in
        \cref{chap:Trust_method}.
  \item \textbf{Lifecycle-based reasoning.} Dataset trustworthiness emerges from the correctness of the processes that produce and transform the data. The framework therefore decomposes evaluation along the four stages of the dataset lifecycle: \emph{sampling}, \emph{collection / measurement}, \emph{annotation}, and \emph{processing}.
  \item \textbf{Multi-granular evaluation.} Some properties are meaningful only at the level of the dataset as a whole (e.g., class balance), others at the level of individual entries (e.g., label correctness), and others at the level of features (e.g., sensor reliability). The framework supports trust sources operating at any of these three granularities and provides aggregation rules between them.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Three-Axis Design Space}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:framework:design-space}

A trust assessment of a training dataset is fully specified by three choices, which together form the design space of the framework. These three choices are formalized as the tuple $(\propP, \propS, \propG)$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\propP$ --- the \textbf{property} being assessed (e.g., accuracy,
        bias, representativeness, privacy);
  \item $\propS$ --- the \textbf{lifecycle stage} from which evidence is
        drawn 
        \\
        ($\propS \in \{\textsc{sampling}, \textsc{collection},
        \textsc{annotation}, \textsc{processing}\}$);
  \item $\propG$ --- the \textbf{granularity} at which the property is
        meaningful 
        \\
        ($\propG \in \{\textsc{dataset}, \textsc{entry},
        \textsc{feature}\}$);
  % \item $\propQ$ --- the \textbf{quantification scheme} mapping evidence to a
  %       subjective opinion 
  %       \\
  %       ($\propQ \in \{\textsc{\acs{BPQ}}, \textsc{\acs{EWQ}},
  %       \textsc{\acs{CUQ}}\}$, defined in
  %       \cref{sec:dataset-trust:formal:quantification}).
\end{itemize}
\noindent Selecting $(\propP, \propS, \propG)$ instantiates a specific assessment problem. The same property may be meaningful at multiple granularities or at multiple stages.

Composite trust proposition such as ''the dataset is reliable for training'' are decomposed by traversing the axes in a fixed order after identifying ''reliability'' as the property $\propP$: first along $\propS$ (the lifecycle stages, i.e., the components of the dataset pipeline being assessed) and then within each stage along $\propG$ (the granularity at which evidence is collected). For example, ''$\dataset$ is reliable for training'' first splits into four stage-level propositions as in \cref{eq:dataset-trust:lifecycle-decomposition}. Each stage-level proposition is then assessed according to which granularity the analyst wants to assess: ''the sampling stage is reliable'' might be a dataset-level sub-proposition about class distribution and a feature-level sub-proposition about coverage; ''the annotation stage is reliable'' might be an entry-level sub-proposition about per-item label correctness and a dataset-level sub-proposition about systematic group-level label bias. Each leaf of the resulting expression is associated with a single trust source from \cref{tab:dataset-trust:catalogue} and quantified with one of the three schemes presented in the next subsection. This fixed traversal order corresponds, in the dataset setting, to the expression-generation phase of the General Methodology (\cref{chap:Trust_method}). 

When an assessment is per-entry or per-feature ($\propG \in \{\textsc{entry}, \textsc{feature}\}$), the same evidence-collection and quantification machinery applies whether the entry comes from the training set or from a single input presented at inference time, as long as the trust sources are available. 

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=gray!10, linewidth=0pt]
\textbf{Relation to \cref{chap:Trust_method}.} The three axes play distinct roles in instantiating the General Trust Assessment Methodology. $\propP$ encodes the \emph{property} to be assessed, which determines what counts as positive or negative evidence. $\propS$ identifies the \emph{lifecycle stage}, acting as the target (trustee) of the trust proposition: it is the component of the pipeline whose reliability is being questioned. $\propG$ sets the \emph{granularity} at which evidence is collected and trust sources are selected. Together, $(\propP, \propS, \propG)$ drive the expression-generation phase: they determine which propositions and trust sources appear in the trust expression, which trust sources attach to each leaf, and how evidence is aggregated.

\noindent \textbf{Example.} Consider assessing whether the annotation stage of dataset $\dataset$ is accurate ($\propP$ = accuracy, $\propS$ = annotation, $\propG$ = entry). The trust proposition is ``the labels of $\dataset$ are correct''. Each entry constitutes an atomic proposition; the trust source is inter-annotator agreement (\ac{IAA}); and the resulting per-entry opinions are aggregated to obtain a dataset-level trust opinion on label accuracy.
\end{mdframed}

\subsection{Specializing the General Methodology to Datasets}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:framework:specialisation}
The three-axis design space $(\propP, \propS, \propG)$  lets us re-state the three phases of the General Trust Assessment Methodology in dataset-specific language.

\paragraph{Phase~I --- Expression generation.} 
When the trust proposition is composite (e.g., ``$\dataset$ is reliable for training''), it is first decomposed along the lifecycle axis $\propS$. Each stage then becomes the target of a simpler trust proposition, and the overall trust is expressed as their conjunction:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}(\dataset, \propP)
= \mathcal{T}_{\text{sampling}} \wedge \mathcal{T}_{\text{collection}}
  \wedge \mathcal{T}_{\text{annotation}} \wedge \mathcal{T}_{\text{processing}}.
\label{eq:dataset-trust:lifecycle-decomposition}
\end{equation}

\noindent The expression is therefore:
\[
T_{\mathcal{T}(\dataset, \propP)} = T_{\mathcal{T}_{\text{sampling}}} \cdot T_{\mathcal{T}_{\text{collection}}} \cdot T_{\mathcal{T}_{\text{annotation}} } \cdot T_{\mathcal{T}_{\text{processing}}}
\]
where (\(\cdot\)) is the \ac{SL} operator for logical AND ($\wedge$) operator.

Note that given how each stage is performed, \ac{TNA-SL} might be used on well defined \aclu{STN} to expand the expression.


\paragraph{Phase~II --- ATO quantification.} 
Each stage-level proposition (simple trust proposition in the methodology) is then supported directly by metric-level trust sources, which all provide evidence for the same stage-level property and are combined with an aggregation operator ($\oplus$):
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathcal{T}_{\text{sampling}}}
= T_{\text{DG}} \oplus T_{\text{MCR}} \oplus T_{\text{GRR}}
  \oplus T_{\text{LTCI}} \oplus \dots,
\label{eq:dataset-trust:metric-aggregation}
\end{equation}
and analogously for every other stage. The full catalog of metrics is given in \cref{tab:dataset-trust:catalogue}. The table lists the trust-source metrics considered in this thesis, organized by lifecycle stage, risk category, and typical granularity ($\dGran$ for dataset, $\eGran$ for entry, $\fGran$ for feature). This table is the practical entry point for instantiating the framework: each metric corresponds to a trust source for a trust proposition in \cref{eq:dataset-trust:lifecycle-decomposition}. \Cref{fig:trust_expression_tree_dataset} shows an example of the final resulting tree including the trust sources. This tree corresponds to the maximal decomposition: the property $\propP$ = reliability draws on every lifecycle stage. For other choices of $(\propP, \propS, \propG)$, the relevant expression is the subtree obtained by pruning the branches that do not apply. A property assessed only at the annotation stage retains only the $T_{\text{annotation}}$ subtree; a property defined exclusively at the entry level (e.g., per-instance label correctness) retains only the metric-level leaves whose granularity is $\eGran$.

\Cref{tab:dataset-trust:catalogue} fixes the \emph{structure} of the assessment: which propositions sit in the tree, which trust source attaches to each leaf, and at which granularity. What remains is the \emph{quantification} of each leaf: (1) how to count the evidence from a trust source and (2) how the evidence counts produced by a trust source become a subjective opinion. For (1), in \cref{sec:dataset-trust:applications:compas,sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb,sec:dataset-trust:applications:cifar10h} we will see how we can count the evidence from a trust source. Regarding (2) we recall the three quantification schemes (\acs{BPQ}, \acs{EWQ}, \acs{CUQ}) introduced in \cref{sec:trust_quant_model}; the analyst picks one per leaf according to the nature of the evidence the trust source produces.
\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{\acf{BPQ},}  
    uncertainty decreases as the total number of positive and negative evidence increases.
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:baseline_quant}
    \begin{aligned}
        b_x &= \frac{r_x}{W + r_x + s_x}, \quad
        d_x = \frac{s_x}{W + r_x + s_x}, \quad
        u_x = \frac{W}{W + r_x + s_x}
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{\acf{EWQ},}  
    introduces a weight $w_x$ to account for uncertainty in addition to positive and negative evidence:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:weight_quant}
    \begin{aligned}
        b_x = \dfrac{r_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}, \quad
        d_x = \dfrac{s_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}, \quad
        u_x = \dfrac{w_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{\acf{CUQ},}  
    maintains a fixed uncertainty level $U$, regardless of the number of evidence points. 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:constant_quant}
    \begin{aligned}
        u_x = U, \quad
        \gamma = \dfrac{1-U}{r_x + s_x}, \quad
        b_x = \gamma r_x, \quad
        d_x = \gamma s_x
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

\end{enumerate}

\noindent The three schemes target different evidence regimes. \ac{BPQ} is the default choice when the metric returns a single observed value with no indication of how confident that value is; the prior weight $W$ governs how quickly residual uncertainty shrinks as evidence accumulates. \ac{EWQ} is appropriate when each piece of evidence comes with its own uncertainty weight. \ac{CUQ} is used when domain policy or trust-source metadata fixes the uncertainty externally; the scheme then distributes the remaining mass between trust mass and distrust mass proportionally to evidence. 

\paragraph{Phase~III --- Expression evaluation.} 
Once the trust opinions at the leaves are quantified and the expression is assembled, the final opinion is obtained by evaluating the expression using the \ac{SL} operators.

\paragraph{On intermediate decomposition by risk category.}
Between the stage level and the metric level, the framework can optionally insert a third level grouping metrics by risk category (e.g., \emph{Sampling Bias}, \emph{Temporal Bias}, \emph{Survivorship Bias} within the sampling stage), as suggested by the structure of \cref{fig:dataset-trust:overview}. This intermediate level might be useful when the analyst wants to assess a stage along several distinct risk axes that should hold simultaneously. In this thesis, we keep the two-level structure (stage $\to$ metrics) for clarity. The extension to three levels is a direct consequence of the framework and is left as an option for future instantiations.

\subsection{Cross-granularity composition of trust sources}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:formal:granularity}

The granularity $\propG$ of the property being assessed and the granularity of the trust sources used to assess it are two related choices. A dataset-level property does not need to be evaluated only by dataset-level trust sources; it can equally be assessed by aggregating evidence collected at finer granularities. Similarly, an entry-level property can rely on feature-level evidence about the columns that compose the entry. Trust sources at a finer granularity than the property can be used, as long as their evidence is aggregated up to the level at which the property is defined. The composition flows naturally upward along the granularity hierarchy:
\[\fGran \to \eGran \to \dGran\]

Two complementary approaches support this upward aggregation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opinion composition.} The property is decomposed unit by unit at the finer granularity. Each unit (feature or entry) is assigned a trust source, which produces an \ac{SL} opinion for that unit. The per-unit opinions are then combined into a coarser-grained opinion using \ac{SL} fusion operators, reflecting the assumption that the units jointly constitute the higher-level.

    \item \textbf{Evidence lifting.} A single trust source produces per-unit outcomes at the finer granularity. A threshold is applied to each outcome: units that satisfy the property contribute positive evidence, and those that do not contribute negative evidence. The resulting counts are then quantified with one of the three schemes of \cref{sec:dataset-trust:formal:quantification} to produce a single opinion at the coarser level.
\end{itemize}
\noindent An entry is a structured combination of features. Both opinion composition and evidence lifting can be applied at any level of the granularity hierarchy, moving evidence upward from features to entries, from entries to the dataset, or directly from features to the dataset. The two steps can also be chained, for instance by first composing feature-level opinions into per-entry opinions and then lifting those to the dataset level.

The following sections demonstrate the framework on three applications. Each application explicitly identifies the cell of the design space it occupies, the quantification scheme it uses, and the publication in which it was originally presented. The applications are presented in the following order: multi-stage first, sampling next, then annotation assessment.

This section presents a generic application of the framework introduced in \cref{sec:dataset-trust:framework}. We define dataset reliability as a trust proposition supported by heterogeneous evidence extracted from multiple stages of the dataset lifecycle, and we formalize the operations that turn metric values into subjective opinions and combine opinions across stages. We recall that as final expression we get the expression depicted in \cref{fig:trust_expression_tree_dataset}. We now focus on understanding how we can quantify the trust opinions from trust sources (Trust opinions at the leaves)\footnote{In the General Methodology, the leaves of the trust expression are \acp{ATO}. Here the assessment is finer: each leaf of the expression tree in \cref{fig:trust_expression_tree_dataset} is an opinion from a trust source, and the resulting fused opinion is what plays the role of the \ac{ATO} in the higher-level expression.}.

This first application demonstrates the framework's ability to compose stage-level assessments into a dataset-level opinion. COMPAS exercises all four lifecycle stages simultaneously.

\subsection{Trust Opinion from Trust Sources metric}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:formal:evidence}
Each lifecycle-stage proposition is supported by a set of trust sources. Trust-source metric values $m$ are first mapped into normalized quality scores
\[
q_m \in [0,1],
\]
where values close to one indicate strong support for the proposition and values close to zero indicate evidence against it. This normalization ensures comparability across heterogeneous metrics.

For metrics where lower values indicate better quality (e.g., error rates), we apply
\begin{equation}
q_m = \max\!\left(0,\, 1 - \frac{m}{\tau_m}\right),
\label{eq:dataset-trust:q-low}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_m > 0$ is a domain-specific tolerance threshold. The formula is a linear ramp: $q_m = 1$ when the observed value is zero (no error), $q_m = 0$ when $m$ reaches the tolerance $\tau_m$, and the score decreases linearly between these two endpoints. Values beyond $\tau_m$ are clamped at zero rather than producing negative quality, since further degradation no longer informs the opinion in any useful way. The choice of $\tau_m$ is therefore the operational definition of \emph{``what bad value is too bad''} for this particular metric. 
Note that a two-threshold form could be applied. However, since such metrics are non-negative by definition, zero is always the ideal value, which removes the need for an explicit lower bound.

For metrics where higher values indicate better quality, we apply
\begin{equation}
q_m = \max\!\left(0,\, \min\!\left(1,\,
        \frac{m - A_m}{L_m - A_m}\right)\right),
\label{eq:dataset-trust:q-high}
\end{equation}
where $A_m$ and $L_m$ denote unacceptable and ideal bounds, respectively. The interpretation mirrors the previous case but with two thresholds rather than one: at $m = A_m$, the metric is unacceptable and contributes no positive support ($q_m = 0$); at $m = L_m$, the metric reaches the ideal level and contributes full positive support ($q_m = 1$); between $A_m$ and $L_m$, the score ramps linearly, so partial achievement yields partial support. Values below $A_m$ or above $L_m$ are clamped to the corresponding endpoint. The two-threshold form is needed because, for higher-is-better metrics, the ``good'' region has both a floor (below which there is no support at all) and a saturation point (above which additional improvement does not add support).

% \marginpar{From Evidence to Subjective Opinions}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:formal:quantification}

To derive trust opinions values $(t, d, u)$, normalized metric scores are converted into positive and negative evidence counts:
\begin{equation}
r_m = K_m \, q_m, \qquad s_m = K_m \, (1 - q_m),
\label{eq:dataset-trust:evidence-counts}
\end{equation}
where $K_m$ is an evidence-strength parameter that fixes the total amount of evidence the metric is assumed to provide, $r_m + s_m = K_m$. Larger values of $K_m$ correspond to trust sources the analyst takes more seriously; smaller values correspond to weaker or less authoritative sources. Under \ac{BPQ} with prior weight $W$, the residual uncertainty contributed by this single metric is $u = W / (W + K_m)$, so $K_m$ is also a parameter that controls the value of the uncertainty. Finally, the per-metric trust opinions are aggregated to obtain the stage-level opinion.

\section{Sampling Stage, Dataset Level: Representativeness on \acs{GTSRB}}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=blue!4, linewidth=0pt]
\textbf{Position in the design space.}

$\propP$ = representativeness (Class balance);

$\propS$ = sampling;\quad $\propG$ = dataset;
% $\propQ \in \{\textsc{\ac{BPQ}}, \textsc{\ac{CUQ}}\}$.
\textbf{Reference:}~\cite{10.1007/978-3-032-19096-3_17}.
\end{mdframed}

The second application instantiates the framework on the \ac{GTSRB} to assess freedom from class-imbalance bias. Class balance is a \emph{dataset-level} property: it is meaningful only when the entire class distribution is observed. The application illustrates two complementary trust sources for the same proposition, one suited to centralized settings and one suited to federated settings, and exercises two of the three quantification schemes of \cref{sec:dataset-trust:formal:quantification}.

\paragraph{Trust proposition.}
\emph{``The dataset $\dataset$ is not biased with respect to class balance.''} We treat this as a simple trust proposition at the dataset-level. The expression tree is therefore a single leaf (\ac{ATO}), and the application focuses on how the leaf is quantified.

% \subsubsection{Class probabilities distribution}
To assess balance, we describe the dataset by its \emph{class probabilities distribution}. For a classification task with $K$ classes and $N$ data points, the empirical probability of class $k$ is
\begin{equation}
P(\mathrm{Class}_k) = \frac{N_k}{N}, \qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(\mathrm{Class}_k) = 1,
\end{equation}
where $N_k$ is the number of data points belonging to class $k$. This distribution is the basic object on which both trust sources operate. A perfectly balanced dataset has all probabilities equal to $1/K$; the density of the class-probability distribution is then concentrated around $1/K$ and approaches a Dirac delta in the large-$N$ limit. Imbalanced
datasets exhibit a wider spread.

\Cref{fig:gtsrb:probability-density} contrasts two illustrative configurations with the same total size ($N = 5200$, $K = 6$): an imbalanced dataset with class counts $[100, 100, 1000, 1000, 1000, 2000]$ and a balanced dataset with class counts $[857, 857, 867, 867, 876, 876]$. The imbalanced case shows a broader probability spread; the balanced case concentrates around the
expected value $1/K = 1/6 \approx 0.167$.

\subsection{Trust source --- Method 1: class-probabilities-based
quantification}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb:method1}

The first trust source operates on the class-probabilities distribution directly. A class is considered \emph{balanced} when its empirical probability lies within a tolerance interval around the expected value:
\begin{equation}
P(\mathrm{Class}_k) \in
  \left[\frac{1}{K} - \eta_1,\ \frac{1}{K} + \eta_2\right].
\label{eq:gtsrb:tolerance-zone}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\eta_1, \eta_2$ control the width of the tolerance zone and play the same role as the tolerance $\tau_m$ in \cref{eq:dataset-trust:q-low}: they define the boundary beyond which a class probability is considered unacceptably imbalanced. We set $\eta = \eta_1 = \eta_2$ for the experiments reported below; the impact of $\eta$ is studied explicitly later in the section.

Positive evidence is the area under the density curve $f$ of the class-probabilities distribution that falls within the tolerance zone, and negative evidence is the complementary area:
\begin{equation}
r = \int_{1/K - \eta_1}^{1/K + \eta_2} f(x)\, dx
  = \frac{n_t}{K},
\qquad
s = 1 - r,
\label{eq:gtsrb:method1-evidence}
\end{equation}
where $n_t$ is the number of classes whose empirical probability falls
within the tolerance zone.

\paragraph{Quantification scheme: \ac{CUQ}.}
The Method-1 evidence is naturally normalized ($r + s = 1$), so the remaining choice is how to set the uncertainty mass. Method~1 measures balance from the empirical class-probabilities distribution, which is itself estimated from the dataset: a \emph{small} dataset gives a noisy estimate, so the trust opinion should be held with low confidence regardless of how balanced the observed distribution appears. The right notion of \emph{small} is relative to the model's data requirements, which is exactly what \emph{sample complexity} measures in PAC-learning theory.

Following Haussler et al.~\cite{HAUSSLER1994248}, the sample complexity required for PAC-learnability is $\mathcal{M}(\epsilon, \delta) = \mathcal{O}\!\left(
  \frac{d}{\epsilon}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)$,
where $d$ is the VC dimension of the model class, $\epsilon$ is the desired accuracy, and $\delta$ is the failure probability. We define the \emph{theoretical} sample complexity as $N_s = \frac{d}{\epsilon}\log(1/\delta)$ and use it to set the \ac{CUQ} uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
U = \min\!\left(
      \max\!\left(
        \frac{\log(N_s) - \log(N)}{10},\ m_1\right),\ m_2\right),
\label{eq:gtsrb:uncertainty}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the actual dataset size and $[m_1, m_2]$ is the desired range of uncertainty (set to $[0,1]$ in our experiments). The intuition is direct: when $N$ is large relative to $N_s$, the dataset is sufficient for the model and the uncertainty in the assessment shrinks toward zero; when $N$ is small relative to $N_s$, the assessment cannot be made with confidence regardless of how balanced the observed distribution is, and the uncertainty is large.

Once $U$ is fixed by \cref{eq:gtsrb:uncertainty}, trust mass and distrust mass follow from the \ac{CUQ} formula ($\gamma = (1-U)/(r+s)$, $b = \gamma r$, $d = \gamma s$).

\subsection{Trust source --- Method 2: entropy-based quantification}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb:method2}
In federated or collaborative settings, no single party observes the global class-probabilities distribution. Each contributing node (e.g., each \ac{OEM} in a vehicular collaborative dataset) only sees its own sub-dataset. Method 1 can be applied with each contributor sharing its class counts. However, this might be undesirable in privacy-sensitive settings. Method 2 takes another path: each node summarizes its sub-dataset with a single scalar, which contributes either to a positive or negative evidence.

\paragraph{Entropy as a balance proxy.}
Each contributor $i$ computes the entropy of its local
class-probabilities distribution $P^{(i)}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}(P^{(i)})
= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p^{(i)}_k \log p^{(i)}_k.
\end{equation}
Entropy is maximal when the local distribution is uniform and minimal when one class dominates.

% \paragraph{Threshold calculation.}
Each local entropy is compared against a threshold $T$ that defines the \emph{edge of the acceptable balance}. We construct an \emph{edge-acceptable} distribution $P_E$ by setting each class probability to $1/K \pm \eta$ (with one class set to exactly $1/K$ when $K$ is odd, so that $P_E$ sums to one), and define
\begin{equation}
T = \mathcal{H}(P_E).
\end{equation}
Contributors whose local entropy exceeds $T$ provide \emph{positive evidence}; the others provide \emph{negative evidence}. Aggregating over the $M$ contributing nodes yields the evidence counts $r$ and $s$ used by the quantification scheme.

\paragraph{Quantification scheme: \ac{BPQ}.}
Each contributor produces one binary piece of evidence (above or below $T$), so the natural quantification is the baseline-prior scheme of \cref{sec:dataset-trust:formal:quantification}: as $M$ grows, the residual uncertainty $u = W / (W + r + s)$ shrinks. This is the desired behavior in a federated setting, where adding more contributors should progressively reduce the uncertainty in the global trust assessment without requiring any party to disclose its local class distribution.

A natural extension treats the distance between each local entropy and $T$ as an evidence weight, which moves the scheme from \ac{BPQ} to \ac{EWQ}: contributors whose local entropy is far from $T$ provide high-weight evidence; contributors near the boundary provide low-weight evidence and contribute proportionally less to the aggregated opinion.

\subsection{Experimental evaluation}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb:eval}

We evaluate both trust sources on the German Traffic Sign Recognition Benchmark~\cite{GTSRB}, which contains over 50,000 images across $K = 43$ traffic-sign classes. The trained model is a \ac{CNN} with three convolutional blocks (64/128/256 filters, $3 \times 3$ kernels) each followed by batch normalization, $2 \times 2$ max-pooling, and dropout (rate $0.25$); two fully connected layers (256 and 128 ReLU units); a final dropout of rate $0.5$; and a softmax output of size 43. Training uses Adam, sparse categorical cross-entropy, batch size 36, and 5 epochs. The expected class probability is $1/K \approx 0.02326$ and the tolerance is fixed at $\eta = 0.02$ for both methods.

We define two scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scenario 1 (centralized).} A single dataset is assessed, in two variants: the original \ac{GTSRB} and an augmented version obtained by oversampling minority classes with SMOTE~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1106-1813}. Method 1 is the only choice in this setting since the global class distribution is fully observable.
  \item \textbf{Scenario 2 (collaborative).} The dataset is partitioned across $M$ \acp{OEM}, in two variants: balanced sub-datasets, and sub-datasets where a configurable number of contributors are intentionally imbalanced (warning signs removed). Both methods are evaluated: Method~1 is applied as follows: each participant shares the distribution of each dataset to an aggregator which will compute the final trust opinion, and Method~2 is applied to the per-contributor summaries without requiring access to the global distribution.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scenario~1: original vs.\ augmented dataset.}
\Cref{fig:gtsrb:distscenario1} compares the class distribution and the class-probabilities distribution of the original and augmented datasets. SMOTE flattens the class distribution and concentrates the probability density around $1/K$. The downstream effect on the model is modest in aggregate accuracy ($95.17\%$ vs.\ $95.10\%$) but visible in the per-group accuracy gap, which drops from $5.24\%$ to $3.88\%$ (\cref{tab:gtsrb:acc1}).

The Method-1 trust opinions reflect the same pattern but make it quantitative: the original dataset is assessed as $(t, d, u) = (0.50,\ 0.11,\ 0.39)$ and the augmented dataset as $(0.64,\ 0,\ 0.36)$. Trust mass rises by $0.14$, distrust mass drops to zero, and the residual uncertainty stays roughly constant because the dataset size is only marginally changed and the \ac{CUQ} uncertainty (\cref{eq:gtsrb:uncertainty}) depends only on $N$.

\subsection{Summary}


The two trust sources answer the same trust proposition by different
routes and exhibit complementary strengths. Method~1 requires the global class-probabilities distribution and is the natural choice when the dataset is centralized and fully observable. Method~2 only requires per-contributor entropy and is therefore the natural choice in collaborative or federated settings, where no party observes the global distribution and class counts cannot be shared. One limitation of Method~2 is worth noting: two locally unbalanced sub-datasets can combine into a globally balanced dataset, yet each contributor would report negative evidence, pulling the trust opinion toward distrust even when the overall distribution is acceptable. Method~1, operating on the global distribution, is immune to this artifact. The two methods should therefore be seen as complementary rather than interchangeable, and the choice between them depends on whether the global distribution is observable.

% \marginpar{Behavior of uncertainty.}
The two methods illustrate two different choices of quantification scheme. Method~1 fixes the uncertainty externally via sample complexity, which is appropriate when the dataset size is the bottleneck on the assessment's confidence. Method~2 lets uncertainty shrink as more contributors provide evidence, which is appropriate when the relevant unit of evidence is one local assessment per contributor. The same proposition can therefore be assessed under \ac{CUQ} or \ac{BPQ} depending on whether ``more evidence'' means ``more data'' or ``more contributors''. Method~1 already avoids access to individual data points by operating on class counts. Method~2 strengthens this property by sharing only a single scalar (local entropy) per contributor, which makes it suitable for privacy-sensitive collaborative settings.

Class imbalance is not always a defect: in some applications it faithfully reflects the deployment-time class frequencies, and forcing balance would degrade rather than improve the model. Trust opinions should therefore be interpreted in light of the deployment context. A natural extension of Method~1 is to replace the uniform expected probability $1/K$ with a deployment-derived target distribution, preserving the rest of the machinery. This application illustrates that the same trust proposition admits multiple ways to be quantified depending on what is observable in the deployment regime.

\section{Annotation Stage, Entry Level: Labeling accuracy on \\CIFAR-10H}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:cifar10h}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=blue!4, linewidth=0pt]
\textbf{Position in the design space.}

$\propP$ = accuracy;
$\propS$ = annotation;\quad
$\propG$ = entry;
% $\propQ \in \{\textsc{\ac{BPQ}}\}$.
\textbf{Reference:}~\cite{trust-aidatatrust}.
\end{mdframed}

The third application addresses labeling accuracy on CIFAR-10H, in which each CIFAR-10 test image is annotated by 50 crowdworkers. Unlike representativeness, labeling accuracy is \emph{per-entry}:  each label decision for each image is treated as a separate trust proposition, and the annotations provided by crowdworkers serve as the evidence for assessing that proposition. This application illustrates how the framework operates at the entry granularity and how the same machinery extends to assessing the reliability of the annotators themselves, which connects directly to the trust-discount operator.

\paragraph{Trust proposition.}
For each image $x$, the proposition is \emph{``the decision label $y^\prime$ for $x$ is accurate.''} The decision label is fixed in advance, typically by majority vote. Each instance is assessed independently, producing a per-entry opinion $\omega_{y^\prime} = (b_{y^\prime}, d_{y^\prime}, u_{y^\prime})$. The set of per-entry opinions can be aggregated into a dataset-level opinion using the cross-granularity rule of \cref{sec:dataset-trust:formal:granularity}.

\subsection{Trust source: divergence to the decision label}
\label{sec:dataset-trust:applications:cifar10h:source}

The trust source operates at the level of individual annotators. For each annotator $j$ of instance $x$, we compute a divergence score $d_j(x)$ that measures how far the annotator's response is from the decision label $y^\prime$. For categorical annotations, $d_j(x) = 0$ when the annotator agrees with $y^\prime$ and $d_j(x) = 1$ otherwise; for probabilistic annotations, \acs{KL} divergence or cross-entropy between the annotator's distribution and $y^\prime$ is a natural choice; for free-text annotations, semantic distance (e.g., cosine similarity between embeddings) plays the same role.

A divergence threshold $\delta$ separates evidence supporting the proposition from evidence against it:
\begin{equation}
r_x = \big|\{j : d_j(x) \leq \delta \}\big|,
\qquad
s_x = \big|\{j : d_j(x) > \delta \}\big|.
\label{eq:cifar10h:evidence}
\end{equation}

The threshold $\delta$ can be set in several ways depending on the annotation modality and on what reference information is available.

\noindent \emph{By edge-case construction}, $\delta$ is set to the divergence that an \emph{edge-acceptable} annotation would produce relative to the decision label: that is, an annotation that is the most distant response still considered acceptable for the given label, by analogy with the edge-acceptable distribution used to set the threshold in Method 2 of the \ac{GTSRB} application (\cref{sec:dataset-trust:applications:gtsrb:method2}).

\noindent \emph{By domain policy}, $\delta$ is fixed by external requirements, e.g., $\delta = 0$ for categorical labels (strict agreement) and $\delta = 0.1$ for soft labels.

\cref{alg:cifar10h:opinions} summarizes the construction of per-entry opinions.


\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Opinion construction from annotations}
\label{alg:cifar10h:opinions}
\label{alg:opinions}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Annotations $\{y_j(x)\}$ or $\{p_j(x)\}$, decision label $y^*$,
threshold $\delta$, prior weight $W$
\For{each instance $x$}
  \State $r_x \leftarrow |\{j : \mathrm{divergence}(y_j(x), y^*) \leq \delta \}|$
  \State $s_x \leftarrow |\{j : \mathrm{divergence}(y_j(x), y^*) > \delta \}|$
  \State $b_x \leftarrow r_x / (W + r_x + s_x)$
  \State $d_x \leftarrow s_x / (W + r_x + s_x)$
  \State $u_x \leftarrow W / (W + r_x + s_x)$
  \State Output opinion $\omega_x = (b_x, d_x, u_x, a)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Quantification schemes: \ac{BPQ} and \ac{EWQ}.}
The trust source produces evidence counts $(r_x, s_x)$ that grow with the number of annotators $n = r_x + s_x$ for instance $x$. This makes two quantification schemes natural. \ac{BPQ}, with prior weight $W$, gives uncertainty $u_x = W / (W + n)$: residual uncertainty decreases as more annotators contribute, which is the desired behavior when annotation volume varies across instances. \ac{EWQ} additionally assumes that each annotation comes with its own uncertainty weight. It is relevant when annotators self-report a confidence score alongside their label, or when external metadata provides a per-annotation reliability proxy (e.g., annotation duration). Where \ac{BPQ} lets the \emph{amount} of evidence drive uncertainty, \ac{EWQ} lets the \emph{quality} of each piece of evidence drive it.


\paragraph{Worked example.}
Consider an instance $x$ annotated by $50$ crowdworkers, of whom $46$ agree with the decision label and $4$ disagree, with $\delta = 0$ and $W = 2$. \cref{eq:cifar10h:evidence} gives $r_x = 46$, $s_x = 4$, and \ac{BPQ} gives:
\begin{equation}
b_x = \tfrac{46}{52} \approx 0.885,\quad
d_x = \tfrac{4}{52} \approx 0.077,\quad
u_x = \tfrac{2}{52} \approx 0.038.
\end{equation}
The opinion captures three qualitatively different signals simultaneously: high trust mass reflecting strong agreement, residual distrust reflecting genuine disagreement among annotators, and low residual uncertainty reflecting that the evidence base is large.

\paragraph{Annotator-level extension}
The same machinery applies to a different entity: the annotators themselves instead of just the annotations. For annotator $j$, partition $j$'s responses into agreements and disagreements with the corresponding decision labels yields evidence counts $(r_j, s_j)$ supporting the proposition \emph{``annotator $j$ is reliable.''}. Applying \ac{BPQ} to $(r_j, s_j)$ produces a per-annotator trust opinion $\omega_j$ that grades annotator quality on the same $(t, d, u)$ scale as the per-entry opinions. 

This construction implicitly assumes that more than half of the participants are honest: the decision label $y^\prime$ is taken as the reference against which each annotator is judged, so the resulting reliability opinion is only meaningful if the procedure that produced $y^\prime$ is itself reliable. If $y^\prime$ is set by majority vote, the assumption is that the majority of annotators on each item is correct on most items. In settings where these assumptions fail (e.g., adversarial annotators colluding to flip the majority on targeted items), $\omega_j$ would conflate genuine reliability with mere conformity to a corrupted reference.

This per-annotator opinion is the input expected by the trust-discount operator. In a stricter assessment, each annotator's contribution to per-entry evidence in \cref{eq:cifar10h:evidence} can be weighted by $\omega_j$ before aggregation: high-reliability annotators contribute fully, low-reliability annotators contribute less.

We evaluate the trust source on CIFAR-10H, which provides around $50$ human annotations per image for the CIFAR-10 test set. The decision label $y^\prime$ for each image is set to the majority annotator response. The divergence threshold is $\delta = 0$ (strict agreement) and the prior weight is $W = 2$ for the \ac{BPQ} experiments. 

\Cref{fig:cifar10h:masses} compares the empirical distribution of $(b_x, d_x, u_x)$ on the full dataset (around \(50\) annotations per image) against a subsampled case where each instance is cropped to 10 annotations by removing 40 responses from the most voted label, so that the cropped set retains the minority annotations while reducing the dominant agreement signal. Two effects are visible. First, the full dataset concentrates trust mass near $1$ and uncertainty near $0$: with around $50$ annotators per image, $u_x = W / (W + 50) \approx 0.04$ regardless of the agreement ratio. This high apparent confidence is partly an artifact of evidence saturation rather than a genuine signal of label quality. Second, the cropped setting redistributes trust mass across the two components: distrust mass becomes visible for items with annotator disagreement and uncertainty rises mechanically because $u_x = W / (W + 10) \approx 0.17$. The opinions are now informative about the actual difficulty of each item, with controversial instances showing simultaneously low trust mass and high distrust.

\section{Summary and Conclusions} \label{sec:dataset-trust:summary}

This chapter introduced a framework for assessing the trustworthiness of \ac{AI} training datasets by specializing the general methodology of \cref{chap:Trust_method}. Its central claim is that dataset trustworthiness should be treated not as a single score, but as a structured assessment defined by three dimensions: the property assessed, the lifecycle stage from which evidence is collected, and the granularity at which it applies. 

The framework is supported by three main artifacts. First, it formalizes dataset reliability through lifecycle decomposition (sampling, collection, annotation, processing), normalization of metrics via thresholds, and adapted quantification schemes (\ac{BPQ}, \ac{EWQ}, \ac{CUQ}). Second, the trust-source catalog provides a practical set of metrics aligned with lifecycle stages and granularity levels. Third, cross-granularity composition rules define how evidence can be aggregated across feature-, entry-, and dataset-levels.

Three applications illustrate the framework. The \ac{COMPAS} case demonstrates multi-stage assessment and aggregation into a dataset-level opinion. The  representativeness study on \ac{GTSRB} shows that the same proposition admits multiple trust sources depending on what is observable in the deployment setting. The labeling accuracy study on CIFAR-10H highlights annotation-stage, entry-level assessment, with extensions toward annotator-level trust.

The framework provides richer decision support than scalar metrics. A dataset-level opinion $(t, d, u)$ distinguishes supporting evidence, contradicting evidence, and uncertainty. This enables auditors to differentiate between lack of evidence and conflicting evidence, two situations requiring different actions, making the framework a diagnostic tool rather than a pass/fail measure.

Three limitations remain. First, the trust-source catalog is not exhaustive and can be extended. Second, hyperparameters (e.g., $\tau_m$, $(A_m, L_m)$, $K_m$, $W$) require manual tuning, though partial calibration appears feasible as illustrated with $\eta$ in the \ac{GTSRB} application. Third, lifecycle stages are treated uniformly, whereas task-dependent weighting may be needed, potentially via discounting operators.

The framework outputs trust opinions at various granularities, which serve as input to the Trust Propagation Module of \ac{PaTAS}, which operates on per-feature trust opinions. Coarser-grained opinions can be replicated at the feature level, enabling propagation through model parameters during training. At inference time, the same process applies to input features, and the resulting per-feature opinions are propagated through the network to produce an output trust opinion. However, replicating a coarser-grained dataset-level opinion as per-feature opinions at the training dataset means every input receives the same trust score regardless of its individual quality, which limits the value of per-instance trust evaluation during inference. For inference-time assessment to be fully meaningful, trust sources that operate at the feature or entry level on the individual input should be preferred.

Finally, while different granularities of assessments may share the same trust property, they do not need to rely on the same trust sources. The analyst is free to use different trust sources for the training dataset and for inference-time inputs, as long as both address the same underlying proposition. For instance, dataset-level accuracy may be supported by sensor-consistency metrics computed across the training set, while input-level accuracy at inference time may be supported by an out-of-range check or a per-sensor confidence score applied to the individual input.
