\chapter{Use Case: \acs{PaTAS} Applied to 5G Network Energy Management}
\label{chap:usecase}
\noindent
The previous chapters introduced the theoretical foundations and system architecture for trust assessment in \ac{NN} systems. In particular, \cref{chap:ovl,chap:dataset-trust,chap:patas} introduced the \ac{PaTAS} architecture for parallel trust assessment through the whole \ac{NN} system pipeline. This chapter demonstrates how these contributions apply in practice through a concrete use case in 5G network energy management. The objective is not to introduce new theoretical contributions, but rather to illustrate how the proposed methodology can be applied to assess trustworthiness within a realistic \ac{ML} scenario. By instantiating the full trust assessment pipeline, from trust proposition definition to opinion quantification and reasoning, we show how the framework can support practical decision-making regarding the trustworthiness of \ac{ML} systems. The code for this chapter is available on \href{https://github.com/Ouatt-Isma/PaTAS-Subjective-Logic-Neural-Networks-Trust-Assessment}{github}.


\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}
5G network energy management is a trust-critical domain: decisions are automated, data arrives from heterogeneous hardware, and errors propagate directly into resource allocation. In this work, we evaluate \ac{PaTAS} using a real-world dataset from 5G network energy consumption monitoring~\cite{netop_5g_energy_2026}. This scenario is particularly suited for evaluating \ac{PaTAS} because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Data comes from heterogeneous infrastructure spanning more than 1,000 base stations with 12 different hardware types, creating natural variation in measurement reliability.
    \item Labels are derived via automated quantile binning, introducing systematic artifacts independent of measurement noise.
    \item Both features and labels carry inherent noise and uncertainty that must be explicitly modeled for trustworthy predictions.
    \item The task has direct practical significance: accurate energy modeling is essential for optimizing resource allocation.
\end{itemize}

Through this use case, we answer four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How should trust opinions be assigned to real-world data?
    \item How do trust opinions propagate through the \ac{NN} during training?
    \item Does the trust signal produced by \ac{PaTAS} reliably indicate prediction correctness under noise?
    \item How does \ac{PaTAS}-based per-prediction trust relate to a global calibration-trust assessment of the same model?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:dataset} describes the 5G energy dataset and experimental setup. \Cref{sec:trust_quantification} presents the trust quantification methodology for features and labels. \Cref{sec:trust_propagation} describes how trust opinions propagate through the \ac{NN} during training. \Cref{sec:noise_robustness} evaluates PaTAS as a confidence signal under noise.  \Cref{sec:calibration_quantification} compares per-prediction trust with global calibration trust. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


\section{Dataset and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:dataset}
\subsection{The 5G Energy Dataset}
\label{sec:5g_dataset}

The 5G energy consumption dataset consists of operational measurements collected from a large-scale production network deployment over an 8-day period. It comprises measurements from more than 1,000 \acp{RRU} and \acp{AAU} spanning 12 distinct hardware product types, collected at hourly granularity.

\subsubsection{Feature Engineering and Label Definition}
\label{sec:features_labels}

Features are constructed through a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline. First, the three CSV files are joined on $(Time, BS, CellName)$. Cell-level measurements are aggregated to base station level by computing the mean of numerical features and the mode of categorical features. The resulting feature vector has 26 dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Load: 1 feature (cell utilization factor)
    \item Energy-saving mode: 6 features (ESMode1 to ESMode6, binary/ordinal indicators)
    \item Hardware characteristics: 5 features (Bandwidth, Frequency, Antennas, TXpower, NumCells)
    \item Equipment type: 14 features (one-hot encoding of 12 RUType variants plus 2 Modes)
\end{itemize}

All numerical features are z-standardized (mean 0, standard deviation 1) before use. The resulting dataset contains close to 100,000 samples. The target label is energy consumption discretized into three classes via quantile binning, computed independently per base station and time window: Low (at or below the 33rd percentile), Medium (between the 33rd and 66th percentile), and High (above the 66th percentile). This automated procedure introduces natural label noise, as measurement errors and temporal shifts cause static quantile boundaries to misalign with true energy classes, a phenomenon we simulate through controlled label flipping, as described in \Cref{sec:noise_injection}.

\subsection{Model Configuration}
\label{sec:model_config}

We train a 2-layer \ac{MLP} with architecture $26 \to 32 \to 3$ to learn the energy classification task. This architecture treats each hourly sample as independent, thereby ignoring the temporal structure inherent in the dataset; architectures better suited to sequential data, such as \ac{LSTM} Networks, are discussed in \Cref{sec:limitations}. We deliberately choose this simple architecture for three reasons: (1) it balances model capacity with interpretability, allowing clear observation of trust phenomena without the confounding of deep feature hierarchies; (2) it aligns with prior work on \ac{PaTAS} (\cref{chap:ovl}), which has focused on a \acf{SeqNN} to enable controlled analysis of trust propagation; and (3) simple models provide a conservative baseline for evaluating whether trust reasoning adds value even in the simplest case.

All hidden neurons use ReLU activations and output neurons use softmax activation. The training uses simple gradient descent with a fixed learning rate of $0.01$ and batch size of 64 for 20 epochs. Batch normalization and dropout are not used, in order to maintain transparency in trust propagation and avoid confounding factors in our analysis.

\subsection{Noise Injection Strategy}
\label{sec:noise_injection}

To evaluate \ac{PaTAS} under realistic data quality variation, we inject controlled noise into both features and labels. Feature noise is injected per-sample: each training sample $i$ independently draws its own noise level $\sigma_i$ from a uniform distribution:

\[
\sigma_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \sigma_{rel}), \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n
\]

Feature noise for sample $i$ is then additive Gaussian:

\[
\tilde{X}_i = X_i + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i \cdot \text{std}(X))
\]

To simulate label errors at controlled levels, we corrupt a fraction $p$ of all training labels by randomly reassigning them to an incorrect class, chosen uniformly from the adjacent classes. This controlled corruption mimics realistic label noise sources such as automated binning failures or inconsistent annotations, while keeping $p$ known by construction so that it can be used directly to compute the label trust opinion. We conduct each experiment $N_{\text{RUNS}} = 5$ times with independent random number generator seeds. Results are averaged to obtain stable mean and standard deviation estimates.

\subsection{Experimental Configurations and Baselines}
\label{sec:configs}
We compare two configurations. The first
% , referred to as \textbf{nn},
is a standard \ac{NN} without any trust reasoning; it establishes the underlying classification performance and serves as the reference for evaluating \ac{PaTAS}. The second
% , referred to as \textbf{ptas-percal},
 is a system where \ac{PaTAS} runs alongside the same \ac{NN} with per-sample heterogeneous trust.
Concretely:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feature trust.} Each training sample $i$ has its trust opinion computed as $\text{feature\_trust}(\sigma_i)$, where $\sigma_i$ is drawn as described above. \ac{PaTAS} looks up $\sigma_i$ by batch index on-the-fly, constructing heterogeneous trust matrices in which each row carries distinct belief, disbelief, and uncertainty values.
  \item \textbf{Label trust.} Computed from the flip probability $p$ via $\text{label\_trust}(p)$.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Experiments, Training and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:exp_axes}

We evaluate the system along three primary axes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature noise:} $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$ with label flip probability $p = 0$.
    \item \textbf{Label flipping:} $p \in  \{0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$ with  $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0$
    \item \textbf{Combined noise:} Selected pairs $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) \in \{(0.10, 0.05), (0.2, 0.15), (0.30, 0.15), (0.30, 0.30)\}$.
\end{enumerate}
For each condition, both configurations are run five times. Reported results are means with standard deviations across runs. The pairing of feature noise and label flipping levels is chosen to reflect realistic scenarios where data quality degrades on multiple dimensions simultaneously.

\paragraph{Training loop.} 
At each training step, the \ac{NN} sends batches of size 64 to the \ac{PaTAS} along with batch indices. The \ac{PaTAS}  retrieves the per-sample trust opinions for that batch and propagates them through the \acf{TNN} in parallel with the standard \ac{NN}'s forward and backward pass. After training, test-time \ac{PaTAS} opinions are computed on the held-out test set under each evaluation condition. We report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Final test accuracy of the baseline \ac{NN}.
    \item Learning curves showing epoch-wise progression.
    \item \ac{PaTAS} opinion masses $(b, d, u)$ on the \ac{NN}'s predicted class, separated by whether the prediction is correct.
    \item Selective-prediction coverage and accuracy under \ac{PaTAS} projected-probability filtering ($\pi = b + u/2$) and softmax-based filtering.
\end{itemize}


\section{Trust Assessment: Assigning Opinions to Data}
\label{sec:trust_quantification}


We quantify trust opinions by mapping noise characteristics directly to binomial opinion components $(b, d, u)$ using two domain-specific functions: one for feature noise, one for label flipping. The trust property being assessed is accuracy: a high-trust opinion indicates that the data is correct. 

\subsection{Feature Trust Quantification}
\label{sec:feature_trust_quantification}

We define the mapping from noise level to uncertainty as the noise-to-information ratio: a noisy measurement carries less information relative to the clean feature value it is meant to represent. After z-standardization, each feature has unit variance by construction, so $\sigma_{\text{rel}}^2 / (1 + \sigma_{\text{rel}}^2)$ is directly interpretable as the ratio of injected noise variance to total variance (noise plus original). Since $\sigma_{\text{rel}} < 1$ by construction, this ratio only reaches $0.5$ at its maximum, leaving the upper half of the uncertainty range $[0, 1]$ unreachable. We therefore apply a factor of 2 to rescale the mapping:

\begin{equation}
u = \frac{2\sigma_{\text{rel}}^2}{1 + \sigma_{\text{rel}}^2}
\label{eq:feature_u}
\end{equation}

The remaining mass $1 - u$ is distributed between belief and disbelief (\ac{CUQ} in \cref{chap:Trust_method}). Positive and negative evidence are taken as:
\begin{equation}
r = 1 - \sigma_{\text{rel}}, \quad s = \sigma_{\text{rel}}
\end{equation}

and normalized by $\gamma = (1 - u) / (r + s) = 1 - u$, giving:

\begin{equation}
b = (1 - \sigma_{\text{rel}}) (1 - u), \quad d = \sigma_{\text{rel}} (1 - u).
\label{eq:feature_bd}
\end{equation}

The rescaled mapping satisfies three desirable properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boundary correctness}: at $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0$ (no noise), $u = 0$, yielding the fully-trusted opinion $(1, 0, 0)$; at $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 1$ (noise power equals signal power), $u = 1$, yielding the vacuous opinion $(0, 0, 1)$.
    \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: $u$ increases strictly with $\sigma_{\text{rel}}$, ensuring that higher noise always produces more uncertainty.
\end{itemize}

Moreover, as measurement noise increases, uncertainty grows as the dominant component, while both belief and disbelief shift: belief decreases from 1 toward 0, and disbelief increases from 0 toward a maximum at $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.5$ before both collapse as $u \to 1$. The net effect is uncertainty-dominant: for $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \leq 1$, uncertainty is always the largest single component, reflecting that noisy measurements reduce confidence without implying systematic deception.




\subsection{Label Trust Quantification}
\label{sec:label_trust_quantification}
Label noise from automated binning is represented by class-flipping errors, where a fraction $p$ of labels are systematically reassigned to the wrong class. Unlike random measurement noise, label flipping is a failure mode of the labeling process itself, increasing the opinion disbelief. 

We quantify label trust through a linear mapping of flip rate to disbelief, with a fixed residual uncertainty $u_0 = 0.2$ reflecting the practical limitation that the exact flip rate $p$ is rarely known with certainty in real deployments. Even when $p$ is estimated from validation data or domain knowledge, some vacuity remains about its precise value. The opinion components are:

\begin{align}
b = (1 - p)(1 - u_0), \quad d = p \cdot (1 - u_0), \quad 
\begin{cases}
    u = u_0 = 0 \, \text{if } p=0\\
    u = u_0 = 0.2 \, \text{if } p>0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:label_bdu}
\end{align}

At $p = 0$ (clean labels), the opinion is $(1, 0, 0)$: fully trusted opinion. As the flip rate increases, belief decreases linearly while disbelief rises symmetrically, both scaled by $(1 - u_0)$. At $p = 1.0$, the opinion becomes $(0, 0.8, 0.2)$, indicating that the labels are reliably wrong, while the residual uncertainty acknowledges that even this assessment carries some doubt.
\paragraph{Comparison of Feature and Label Trust Quantification}
\label{sec:trust_comparison}
The fixed $u_0 = 0.2$ reflects the practical limits of label quality estimation. Unlike feature noise, whose vacuity arises because individual measurement errors are random (even knowing $\sigma_i$, we cannot determine whether any specific feature value is too high or too low), label flipping is deterministic in effect: a flipped label is definitively wrong. However, the flip rate $p$ is itself an estimate derived from automated binning diagnostics or domain expertise, and carries its own uncertainty. The residual $u_0$ encodes this gap, while the remaining mass $(1 - u_0)$ is partitioned linearly between belief and disbelief according to $p$. The two functions thus embody distinct failure modes: feature noise produces uncertainty-dominant opinions converging to $(0, 0, 1)$ as $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \to 1$, while label flipping produces disbelief-dominant opinions bounded by a constant uncertainty floor, converging to $(0, 0.8, 0.2)$ as $p \to 1$. This allows \ac{PaTAS} to reason differently about each source at inference time: measurement uncertainty increases overall doubt while preserving some confidence, whereas label errors produce concrete disbelief proportional to the estimated flip rate.

These input opinions are then propagated through the trained \ac{NN} for parameter trust quantification described in \Cref{sec:trust_propagation}. The benefit of this propagation is that the output opinion on each predicted class reflects both the quality of the input and the accumulated evidence about parameter reliability acquired during training. Because \ac{PaTAS} leaves the \ac{NN}'s predictions unchanged, the relevant evaluation question is not the magnitude of the output opinion in isolation, but whether it is informative about whether the prediction is accurate. The following section answers this question.


\section{Trust Propagation: How Opinions Evolve During Training in \ac{PaTAS}}
\label{sec:trust_propagation}

The previous section described how trust opinions are assigned at the data level. This section describes how these opinions propagate through the \ac{NN} during training, producing trust opinions on the \ac{NN}'s predictions. The mechanisms described here operationalize the Trust Propagation part of the \ac{PaTAS} framework introduced in \cref{chap:patas}: the use case instantiates the Trust Feedforward and Parameter-Trust Update modules on the 5G energy classification task.

Several \ac{SL} operators drive trust propagation in \ac{PaTAS}: \emph{trust discounting} and \emph{belief fusion} for forward propagation, and \emph{deduction} combined with \emph{belief fusion} for the trust update applied during backpropagation.


\subsection{Trust Discounting and Fusion for Forward Propagation}
\label{sec:discounting_forward}

When trust opinions flow from inputs through the network, they encounter weight opinions $\omega_{\theta_{i,j}^{(l)}}$ that represent the learned trust in each parameter $\theta_{i,j}^{(l)}$, the weight connecting neuron $j$ in layer $l-1$ to neuron $i$ in layer $l$. Trust propagation through a single Trust Node mirrors the structure of the corresponding neuron: where the neuron computes a weighted sum followed by an activation, the Trust Node replaces multiplication by discounting and summation by fusion.

Concretely, for each neuron $i$ in layer $l$, the Trust Function computes:

\begin{equation}
T_{z^{(l)}_i} = \bigoplus_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} T_{x^{(l-1)}_j} \otimes T_{\theta^{(l)}_{i,j}}, 
\qquad 
T_{x^{(l+1)}_i} = T_{\varphi^{(l)}}\!\left(T_{z^{(l)}_i}\right)
\label{eq:trust_function}
\end{equation}

where $\otimes$ is the trust discounting operator, $\bigoplus$ denotes the fusion operator aggregating contributions from all predecessors $\mathcal{N}(i)$, and $T_{\varphi^{(l)}}$ is the trust-equivalent of the activation function, set to the identity in this work since we use \ac{ReLU} activations~(\cref{chap:patas}).

The two operations have complementary roles:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trust discounting} ($\otimes$) models the dependency of a downstream component on an upstream one. An input opinion $T_{x_j}$ discounted by parameter trust $T_{\theta_{i,j}}$ yields a refined opinion reflecting that the reliability of the parameter limits how much of the input's trustworthiness can be passed on. If the parameter is fully trusted $(1,0,0)$, the input opinion is passed through unchanged; if the parameter is fully distrusted $(0,1,0)$, the discounted opinion collapses to vacuous regardless of the input.
    \item \textbf{Belief fusion} ($\bigoplus$) aggregates contributions from multiple predecessors into a single opinion. Since each input $x_j$ provides correlated evidence for the neuron's output, averaging fusion is the appropriate operator.
\end{itemize}

This two-step process propagates layer by layer from inputs to outputs, with weight opinions $\omega_\theta$ initialized to the vacuous state $(0, 0, 1)$ before training begins. The feedforward procedure is summarized in Algorithm~5 of \cref{chap:patas}.

At the output layer, the aggregated trust opinion for the predicted class is further refined using the underlying \ac{NN}'s predicted class probability. Concretely, for a predicted class with softmax score $\hat{p}$, the output opinion $(b, d, u)$ is transformed as:
\begin{equation}
b' = \hat{p} \cdot b, \quad
d' = (1 - \hat{p}) \cdot d, \quad
u' = \hat{p} \cdot (1 - \hat{p}) \cdot u,
\label{eq:bdu_nnscore}
\end{equation}
normalized by $b' + d' + u'$. This transformation weights the trust opinion by the \ac{NN}'s own confidence in its prediction: a high softmax score amplifies belief and suppresses disbelief, while a low softmax score does the opposite. The result is a composite opinion that combines the trust signal propagated through the \ac{TNN} with the \ac{NN}'s predictive confidence, grounding the final opinion in both the data quality evidence accumulated during training and the model's confidence.

\subsection{Deduction and Fusion for Parameter Updates}
\label{sec:deduction_fusion}

During backpropagation, gradient information is converted into trust evidence to update the weight opinions $\omega_{\theta_{i,j}^{(l)}}$. The update mirrors the structure of standard gradient descent (\cref{eq:gd1} in \cref{chap:patas}): just as the gradient $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \theta_{i,j}^{(l)}$ factors into an error term $\delta_i^{(l)}$ and an input activation $x_j^{(l-1)}$, the Parameter-Trust Update incorporates gradient evidence, label trust, and input feature trust as analogous sources of evidence. The procedure, detailed in Algorithm~6 of \cref{chap:patas}, proceeds in three stages per neuron:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gradient-to-opinion conversion.} For neuron $n_i^{(l)}$, the gradients $\{g_{i,j}^{(l)}\}$ of its incoming weights are classified by magnitude against a sensitivity threshold $\epsilon = 0.05$: weights with $|g_{i,j}^{(l)}| < \epsilon$ contribute positive evidence $r$ (the gradient is small, indicating the parameter is near a reliable optimum), and weights with $|g_{i,j}^{(l)}| \geq \epsilon$ contribute negative evidence $s$. These counts are mapped to a binomial opinion $T_{n_i \mid y_{\text{batch}}}$ via \ac{BPQ}.

    \item \textbf{Deduction against label trust.} The gradient opinion is conditioned on the batch label trust $T_{y_{\text{batch}}}$ using the inferential deduction operator $\circledcirc$, yielding a deduced neuron trust $T_{n_i \| Y_{\text{batch}}}$. This step accounts for the fact that gradient signals are only meaningful insofar as the labels driving them are themselves trustworthy: a strong gradient driven by a mislabeled sample should not increase parameter trust.

    \item \textbf{Revision and adjustment of parameter trust.} The weight opinion $T_{\theta_{i,j}^{(l)}}$ is first revised using $T_{n_i \| Y_{\text{batch}}}$, then adjusted by the joint influence of input feature trust $T_{x_j^{(l-1)}}$ and label trust $T_{y_{\text{batch}}}$. This adjustment reflects Eq.~(7.6) of \cref{chap:patas}: parameter updates depend jointly on gradient signals, input activations, and label quality, so parameter trust must account for all three.
\end{enumerate}

The net effect is that parameter trust accumulates evidence across mini-batches, converging as training progresses. Under clean conditions, weight opinions converge toward high belief; under noisy inputs or labels, trust accumulation is modulated by the degraded input and label opinions, leaving elevated uncertainty or disbelief in the affected weights.


\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl_details_propagation}

The \ac{PaTAS} server and \ac{NN} client communicate via \acs{TCP} sockets, realizing the \ac{NN} Interface described in \cref{chap:patas}. The interaction at each training step proceeds as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The client sends a \texttt{TRAINING\_FEEDFORWARD} message containing the current batch indices.
    \item The \ac{PaTAS} server retrieves the per-sample noise levels $\sigma_i$ stored in \texttt{sigma\_arr}, constructs the heterogeneous input trust array $T_x$ (one opinion per sample per feature), and applies the Trust Feedforward to propagate trust through both layers of the \ac{TNN}, storing intermediate trust scores for use in the update step.
    \item After the client computes gradients, it sends a \texttt{TRAINING\_BACKPROPAGATION} message containing $\delta_W$, $\delta_b$, and the true labels $y$.
    \item The \ac{PaTAS} server constructs the batch label trust opinion $T_{y_{\text{batch}}}$ via $\text{label\_trust}(p)$, runs the Parameter-Trust Update (\cref{alg:trustupdate} in \cref{chap:patas}) to revise $\omega_\theta$ for each layer, and sends an acknowledgment.
\end{itemize}

By design, the trust feedforward and parameter update run in parallel with the standard \ac{NN} computation without modifying it: the \ac{NN}'s weights, gradients, and predictions are unaffected by the trust reasoning. \ac{PaTAS} operates as a confidence signal, not as a regularizer or a modifier of learning dynamics.

\section{Noise Robustness: \ac{PaTAS} as a Confidence Signal Under Data Quality Variation}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

This section evaluates \ac{PaTAS} in its intended role: providing a trust opinion that signals the reliability of \ac{NN} predictions on a held-out test set where every sample receives an independently drawn noise level $\sigma_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, simulating realistic deployment conditions in a 5G network where measurements arrive with heterogeneous quality. Rather than asking whether \ac{PaTAS} improves classification accuracy (by design, it does not modify the \ac{NN}'s predictions), we ask: \emph{can the trust opinion produced by \ac{PaTAS} be used to selectively improve accuracy on retained predictions?} If the trust signal discriminates reliably between correct and incorrect predictions, it can support downstream decision-making by filtering out low-confidence samples and improving accuracy on the subset that passes the filter.

\label{sec:baseline_nn}

As expected the classification accuracy in the clean setting is moderate rather than state-of-the-art; this is intentional, as the goal of this use case is not to maximize accuracy but to evaluate whether \ac{PaTAS} trust opinions provide reliable confidence signals under data quality variation.

The figures reveal qualitatively different degradation profiles under the two noise sources. Under feature noise (\Cref{fig:feature_noise_accuracy}), accuracy degrades continuously but non-linearly: it declines only modestly from 84.9\% to approximately 83.2\% across the low-noise range $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \in [0, 0.30]$ evaluated in the main experiments, then accelerates beyond $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.50$, reaching 74\% at the maximum noise level. This gradual degradation is consistent with the nature of additive Gaussian noise: as $\sigma_{\text{rel}}$ increases, the discriminative information per feature decreases smoothly, causing a proportional but not catastrophic loss of classification performance.

Under label flipping (\Cref{fig:label_noise_accuracy}),the degradation profile is qualitatively different. Accuracy remains nearly flat at 84-85\% from $p = 0$ up to $p = 0.60$, indicating that the model is remarkably robust to moderate label corruption. Beyond this point, accuracy collapses catastrophically: it drops to approximately 7\% at $p = 0.70$ and reaches 3\% from $p = 0.80$ onward.  This phase-transition-like behavior reflects a fundamental threshold in the label corruption process: below $p = 0.5$, the majority of training labels remain correct and the model can still learn an approximately correct decision boundary. Once $p$ exceeds $0.5$, incorrect labels outnumber correct ones and the model is effectively trained on an inverted supervision signal, causing systematic misclassification. However, for $p \in [0.5, 0.6]$ the model retains reasonable performance, since the corruption does not follow any systematic logic that the model could learn to exploit. The sharp collapse near $p = 0.70$ rather than exactly at $p = 0.50$ is consistent with the three-class label structure: when a label is flipped, it is reassigned to one of the (up to two) adjacent wrong classes, which shifts the collapse threshold upward relative to the binary case. 

The experimental range used in the main trust assessment evaluation, $p \in \{0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$, lies entirely within the flat region where accuracy is preserved, which is precisely the regime where trust discrimination is most meaningful: the model is still producing useful predictions, and the trust signal can identify which ones to rely on.

\subsection{\ac{PaTAS} as a Discriminator of Correct Predictions}
\label{sec:patas_discriminator}

The central empirical question is whether \ac{PaTAS} opinions distinguish between correct and wrong \ac{NN} predictions. We compute the mean opinion components $(\bar{b}, \bar{d}, \bar{u})$ in the \ac{NN}'s predicted class, separately for samples where the prediction is correct and where it is wrong. A useful confidence signal should exhibit three simultaneous patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Higher belief on correct predictions: $\bar{b}_c \ge \bar{b}_w$, i.e., $\Delta b =\bar{b}_c - \bar{b}_w  \ge 0$.
    \item Lower uncertainty on correct predictions: $\bar{u}_c \le \bar{u}_w$, i.e., $\Delta u =\bar{u}_c - \bar{u}_w  \le 0$.
    \item Lower disbelief on correct predictions: $\bar{d}_c \le \bar{d}_w$, i.e., $\Delta d =\bar{d}_c - \bar{d}_w  \le 0$.
\end{itemize}

More generally, as noise increases, belief on correct predictions decreases and uncertainty increases, while disbelief remains consistently equal zero. Disbelief equals zero because the feature trust passed to the feedforward is fully trusted ($1,0,0$), leading to zero disbelief.

\paragraph{Feature Noise.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.10$, \ac{PaTAS} belief on correct predictions is substantially higher than on wrong predictions ($\Delta b = +0.228$). 
    \item At $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.20$, discriminability reaches its peak ($\Delta b = +0.237$), suggesting that moderate noise sharpens the contrast between correct and incorrect predictions by increasing the overall uncertainty budget that \ac{PaTAS} can differentially allocate.
    \item At $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.30$, discriminability remains strong and comparable to the low-noise regime ($\Delta b = +0.232$), indicating that the trust signal degrades gracefully rather than collapsing as noise increases within the evaluated range.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Label Flipping.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At $p = 0.05$, the discriminative gap peaks at $\Delta b = +0.140$ with a symmetric uncertainty gap ($\Delta u = -0.140$), and disbelief remains negligible across both correct and incorrect predictions ($\Delta d \approx 0$). This symmetry between $\Delta b$ and $\Delta u$ reflects the structure of the label trust opinion: with $u_0 = 0.2$ fixed, the opinion mass available for discrimination is constrained, and the signal manifests primarily as a clean belief-uncertainty trade-off.
    \item At $p = 0.15$, the gap falls to $\Delta b = +0.120$, indicating faster degradation than under feature noise at comparable corruption levels, consistent with the corruption of the supervision signal itself.
    \item At $p = 0.30$, the gap reaches $\Delta b = +0.041$, reflecting that as more training labels are flipped, the parameter trust accumulated during training becomes less reliable, reducing the ability of \ac{PaTAS} to differentiate correct from incorrect predictions at test time.
\end{itemize}

At $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.30, 0.15)$, the discriminative gap continues to narrow, reaching $\Delta b = +0.084$.  At the most severe condition $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.30, 0.30)$ it reaches $\Delta b = +0.043$, with elevated uncertainty dominating both correct and incorrect predictions. This is consistent with the additive degradation expected when two independent noise sources simultaneously reduce the reliability of both input and label evidence available to the trust propagation mechanism.

A useful signal should achieve high accuracy at low coverage (retaining only the most trustworthy predictions) and degrade gracefully as coverage increases toward 100\% (the unfiltered baseline). \subsection{Summary of Robustness Findings}
\label{sec:robustness_summary}

Three conclusions follow from the empirical results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \ac{PaTAS} belief discriminates between correct and wrong predictions across all evaluated noise conditions. Under feature noise, the discriminative gap $\Delta b$ ranges from $+0.228$ at $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.10$ to $+0.232$ at $\sigma_{\text{rel}} = 0.30$, remaining remarkably stable across the evaluated range rather than degrading monotonically. Under label noise, $\Delta b$ peaks at $+0.140$ at $p = 0.05$ and falls to $+0.041$ at $p = 0.30$, indicating faster degradation consistent with the corruption of the supervision signal itself. Under combined noise, $\Delta b$ falls from $+0.173$ at $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.10, 0.05)$ to $+0.043$ at $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.30, 0.30)$, reflecting the additive effect of both corruption sources.
    
    \item The assessment is consistent with the trust quantification design. Under feature noise, disbelief remains zero throughout, so the discriminative signal is fully captured by $\Delta b$ alone: since $\Delta b + \Delta d + \Delta u = 0$ and $\Delta d = 0$, uncertainty absorbs the complement. This confirms that feature noise is correctly encoded as epistemic uncertainty rather than evidence of deception. Under label flipping, the gap degrades more rapidly as $p$ increases, consistent with corruption of the supervision signal itself; disbelief also remains near zero because the feature trust passed to the feedforward is fully trusted, leaving no pathway for disbelief to accumulate in the propagated opinion.
    
    \item Used as a selective prediction filter, \ac{PaTAS} provides a consistent coverage-accuracy advantage over softmax filtering across all evaluated conditions. The advantage is largest at low to moderate coverage and low to moderate noise, where the trust signal retains strong discriminative power. It is more pronounced under label noise than feature noise, reflecting the sensitivity of the parameter trust update to label quality during training. Under combined noise at $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.10, 0.05)$, \ac{PaTAS} achieves approximately 94\% accuracy at 20\% coverage versus 86\% for softmax, the largest observed gap across all conditions. The advantage degrades gracefully as noise intensifies, converging toward softmax performance at the most severe condition $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.30, 0.30)$, where both signals carry limited discriminative information.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Calibration Quantification using Subjective Logic}
\label{sec:calibration_quantification}
The previous section evaluated \ac{PaTAS} as a per-prediction confidence signal, showing that its trust opinions discriminate between correct and wrong predictions and can be used to improve accuracy through selective filtering. We now turn to a complementary question: \emph{can we produce a single trust opinion that characterizes the trustworthiness of an entire trained model?} This is the calibration-trust problem, addressed using \acf{BPQ} with cumulative fusion across probability clusters, as developed in \cref{chap:bb}.

Unlike \ac{PaTAS}, calibration quantification operates post-hoc on the predictions of an already-trained \ac{NN}. The two methods are complementary: \ac{PaTAS} provides per-sample trust during inference; calibration quantification provides a global trust opinion on the model itself. Evaluating both under the same noise conditions allows us to verify that subjective logic captures different aspects of trustworthiness at different granularities in a consistent manner.


\subsection{Methodology Recap}
\label{sec:calibration_methodology}

The calibration-trust algorithm (\cref{alg:1} in \cref{chap:bb}) computes a single binomial opinion $(b, d, u)$ on the trained \ac{NN} as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clustering predictions.} The predicted probabilities for each class are partitioned into $M = 10$ bins over $[0, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Per-cluster evidence.} For each bin $i$ containing $n_i$ samples with representative probability $\text{RP}_i$, positive evidence is the observed number of correct classifications $t_i$, and negative evidence is $|t_i - n_i \cdot \text{RP}_i|$, the absolute deviation between the observed correct count and the $n_i \cdot \text{RP}_i$ correct predictions expected from a perfectly calibrated model. A well-calibrated bin contributes little negative evidence; a miscalibrated bin where predicted confidence diverges from empirical accuracy contributes proportionally more.
    \item \textbf{Per-cluster opinion.} A binomial opinion is computed via \ac{BPQ} with uncertainty weight $W = 2$.
    \item \textbf{Cumulative fusion.} Cluster opinions are fused per class, then across classes, yielding a single calibration-trust opinion for the \ac{NN}.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure quantifies how well the model's predicted probabilities align with its actual accuracy: a perfectly calibrated model receives an opinion approaching $(1, 0, 0)$, while a miscalibrated model shows elevated disbelief.

\paragraph{Feature noise has limited impact on calibration.}
As $\sigma_{\text{rel}}$ increases from $0$ to $0.5$, belief decreases only modestly from $0.920$ to $0.790$, while disbelief rises from $0.080$ to $0.210$. The model remains well-calibrated despite degraded inputs, indicating that its confidence values continue to track empirical accuracy even when the underlying data is noisier. This is consistent with the graceful accuracy degradation observed in \Cref{sec:baseline_nn}. The limited impact of feature noise on calibration also has a principled interpretation: additive Gaussian noise on features acts analogously to stochastic input perturbations used in differential-privacy-aware training~\cite{Abadi_2016}, which are known to reduce overconfidence by preventing the model from memorizing exact training samples. Feature noise therefore does not break the alignment between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy; if anything, moderate noise can regularize overconfident predictions, which explains why calibration trust degrades slowly even as classification accuracy declines.


\paragraph{Label flipping causes substantial miscalibration.}
Label flipping has a much stronger effect: at $p = 0.30$, belief drops to $0.680$ and disbelief rises to $0.320$. When a systematic fraction of training labels are incorrect, the model learns to assign high confidence to wrong predictions, directly breaking the alignment between predicted probabilities and true accuracy that calibration trust measures. Calibration trust correctly identifies label flipping as the more severe form of corruption, which is consistent with the intuition that label errors corrupt the supervision signal itself, whereas feature noise degrades only the input representation.


\paragraph{Combined noise compounds the effect.}
Under combined corruption, calibration trust degrades faster than under either source alone. At $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.30, 0.30)$, belief falls to $0.654$, the lowest across all conditions. The dominant driver is label flipping, with feature noise contributing additively but secondarily, consistent with the asymmetry observed in the two preceding paragraphs.


\paragraph{Uncertainty remains negligible.}
Across all conditions, uncertainty $u$ stays near $10^{-4}$. With a large test set distributed across $M = 10$ bins and 3 classes, the evidence weight $W = 2$ becomes negligible relative to the cumulative evidence, driving $u \to 0$. The calibration-trust opinion therefore carries almost no residual vacuity: any ambiguity about the model's calibration has been resolved by the volume of predictions observed, and the remaining mass is cleanly partitioned between belief (the model is well-calibrated) and disbelief (it is not).

The two assessments complement each other along two axes.

\textbf{Granularity.} \ac{PaTAS} opinions vary per sample: two inputs processed by the same model receive different trust opinions depending on their individual noise levels. Calibration trust produces a single opinion for the entire model (or for clusters of predicted probability output), summarizing how reliably its confidence scores correspond to its empirical accuracy across the test set. The two granularities serve different decision-making needs: per-prediction trust supports runtime filtering, while model-level trust supports deployment decisions and periodic auditing.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} The two methods are sensitive to different sources of degradation. \ac{PaTAS} is most informative under feature noise, where per-sample uncertainty grows with $\sigma_i$ and the discriminative gap between correct and wrong predictions is sharpest. Calibration trust is most sensitive to label flipping, which corrupts the supervision signal and breaks the confidence-accuracy alignment that calibration measures. This complementary sensitivity means that the two assessments together provide a more complete diagnostic than either alone: a model that passes the calibration-trust check but generates high-uncertainty \ac{PaTAS} opinions may be globally well-calibrated but operating on unreliable inputs; conversely, a model with low calibration trust but reasonable per-prediction \ac{PaTAS} opinions may be systematically overconfident at the model level despite producing locally interpretable predictions.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{What \ac{PaTAS} Adds Beyond Softmax Confidence}
\label{sec:discussion_softmax}

Softmax probabilities collapse confidence into a single scalar, while \ac{PaTAS} produces a three-component opinion $(b, d, u)$. This distinction is meaningful in three respects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty separation.} \ac{PaTAS} distinguishes between low confidence due to insufficient evidence (high $u$, low $d$) and low confidence due to actively conflicting evidence (low $u$, high $d$). Softmax conflates these into a single low-probability output, losing the information needed to decide whether to collect more data or to audit the labeling process.
    \item \textbf{Traceable origins.} Because \ac{PaTAS} opinions are constructed by propagating calibrated input opinions through the Trust Nodes Network, uncertainty in a prediction can be traced back to specific noisy inputs or unreliable parameters. Softmax provides no such provenance.
    \item \textbf{Noise-aware grounding.} The trust opinion encodes explicit knowledge of data quality: the noise level $\sigma_i$ of each training sample is mapped to a calibrated opinion via $\text{feature\_trust}(\sigma_i)$ and propagated all the way to the output. Softmax probabilities have no access to this provenance information and treat all predictions as equally grounded in the training data.
\end{itemize}

These properties explain the empirical pattern observed in  \Cref{sec:selective_prediction}: \ac{PaTAS} provides a consistent coverage-accuracy advantage over softmax filtering across all evaluated conditions, largest at low to moderate coverage where the trust signal retains strong discriminative power. The advantage is most pronounced under combined noise at $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.10, 0.05)$, where \ac{PaTAS} achieves approximately 94\% accuracy at 20\% coverage versus 86\% for softmax. Against a softmax filter at matched coverage, \ac{PaTAS} performs comparably under low noise but maintains a meaningful advantage as noise intensifies, where the noise-provenance signal encoded in the trust opinion becomes the decisive factor.


\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:computational_cost}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{img/usecase/latency.pdf}
    \caption{Inference latency overhead of \ac{PaTAS} across batch sizes from 1 to 1024, measured over 200 repetitions per configuration. Left axis: median latency in milliseconds for \ac{NN} only (black) and \ac{NN} + \ac{PaTAS} (purple). Right axis: overhead ratio (PaTAS / NN, dashed).}
    \label{fig:patas_latency}
\end{figure}

We measured the inference latency overhead of running \ac{PaTAS} alongside the standard \ac{NN} across batch sizes from 1 to 1024, using 200 repetitions per configuration. Results are summarized in \Cref{fig:patas_latency}. We report inference latency as the primary cost metric: training overhead is dominated by the \ac{TCP} socket protocol used in the current client-server implementation, which introduces communication latency independent of the trust computation itself, and therefore does not reflect the intrinsic cost of \ac{PaTAS}. Inference latency, by contrast, measures only the trust feedforward through the \ac{TNN} and is representative of the overhead that would be incurred in a production deployment.

\paragraph{Absolute latency.}
At all evaluated batch sizes, the combined \ac{NN} + \ac{PaTAS} latency remains well under 0.1\,ms up to batch size 64 (approximately 0.09\,ms), rising to approximately 0.15\,ms at batch 128, 0.31\,ms at batch 256, 0.51\,ms at batch 512, and 0.84\,ms at batch 1024. These values are well within the range acceptable for most real-time and near-real-time deployment scenarios.

\paragraph{Overhead ratio.}
The \ac{PaTAS}-to-\ac{NN} overhead ratio is approximately $5.0\times$ at batch size 1, $4.4\times$ at batch 8, and rises back to $5.0\times$ at batch 64. A pronounced dip occurs at batch 128 ($2.6\times$), where vectorized computation over the batch amortizes the per-call fixed cost of the trust feedforward, causing the \ac{NN} forward pass to scale more steeply than \ac{PaTAS}. At larger batch sizes the ratio partially recovers to $3.9\times$ at batch 256 before stabilizing at approximately $2.9\times$ at batch 512 and 1024, reflecting the growth of the trust discounting computation: each sample carries a $(b, d, u)$ triple that must be discounted by the weight opinion at every neuron and accumulated across inputs, which scales less favorably than a standard matrix multiplication at large batch sizes.

\paragraph{Implications for deployment.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-batch and streaming inference is practical.} At batch sizes $\leq 64$, \ac{PaTAS} adds under 0.1\,ms in absolute terms. The approximately $5\times$ relative overhead is the price of a full parallel trust signal; the absolute cost is negligible within typical service-level budgets.

    \item \textbf{Large-batch inference is also feasible.} Even at batch size 1024, the total latency remains below 1\,ms and the overhead ratio stabilizes at approximately $2.9\times$, making batched offline scoring with \ac{PaTAS} practical for periodic auditing or selective prediction over large datasets.

    \item \textbf{Further optimization is straightforward.} The current implementation communicates via \ac{TCP} sockets, adding protocol overhead at training time. Replacing this with shared-memory or in-process integration would reduce both training and inference overhead substantially, and is left as an engineering improvement for production deployments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Label flip rate availability.} The label trust quantification function $\text{label\_trust}(p)$ assumes that the flip rate $p$ is known, or can be reliably estimated. In practice this may not be possible: automated binning diagnostics or holdout validation can provide an estimate of $p$, but that estimate carries its own uncertainty. The fixed residual vacuity $u_0 = 0.2$ adopted in this work partially addresses this by encoding a conservative epistemic gap, but in deployments where $p$ is poorly characterized, the label trust opinion should be assigned with higher uncertainty to reflect this additional source of ignorance.

    \item \textbf{Filter threshold selection.} The coverage-accuracy results in \Cref{sec:selective_prediction} demonstrate that \ac{PaTAS} provides a reliable signal for selective prediction, but they do not prescribe how to choose the operating threshold in practice. The optimal threshold depends on the downstream cost structure: the relative cost of acting on a wrong prediction versus abstaining from a correct one. In this work we report the full coverage-accuracy curve rather than a single operating point, leaving threshold selection to the application context. A principled approach would be to derive the threshold from a decision-theoretic analysis that incorporates the specific cost structure of the deployment domain.

    \item \textbf{Network depth.} We use a 2-layer \ac{MLP}. Trust propagation in deeper architectures may exhibit different dynamics, as trust discounting compounds across layers and residual connections introduce non-sequential trust paths that require the \ac{DSPG} synthesis of \cref{chap:patas} to handle correctly.

    \item \textbf{Assumptions on noise distributions.} The trust quantification functions are designed for the specific noise models used in our experiments. Real-world noise may not follow Gaussian or flip-rate distributions, and the quantification functions should be adapted to the application domain before deployment.

    \item \textbf{Model architecture and temporal structure.} The 5G energy dataset has an inherent temporal structure: energy consumption at a base station at time $t$ is influenced by its recent history of load and energy-saving mode activations. A 2-layer \ac{MLP} treats each hourly sample as independent, discarding this sequential dependency. Architectures better suited to temporal data, such as \ac{LSTM} Networks or Transformer-based sequence models, would likely achieve higher classification accuracy on this task. However, extending \ac{PaTAS} to recurrent architectures is non-trivial: \ac{LSTM} Networks introduce gated state transitions whose trust semantics are not directly captured by the feedforward discounting and fusion operators described in \cref{chap:patas}, and would require a dedicated treatment of how trust flows through recurrent connections. The 
    \ac{MLP} baseline was therefore chosen deliberately to provide a controlled, interpretable setting that isolates the contribution of trust reasoning from architectural effects.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Connection to the Broader Thesis}
\label{sec:discussion_thesis}

This use case demonstrates the practical applicability of the contributions introduced in earlier chapters:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The trust quantification methodology of \cref{chap:dataset-trust} is operationalized through the trust quantification functions for feature noise and label flipping, showing how dataset-level trust assessment translates into concrete per-sample opinions.
    \item The Trust Propagation Module of \cref{chap:patas} is realized end-to-end through the \ac{TCP}-socket client-server interaction, validating the architectural design on a real dataset with controlled noise.
    \item The calibration trust methodology of \cref{chap:bb} provides a complementary global trust assessment that is consistent with the per-prediction findings: both methods identify label flipping as the more damaging corruption source, and both degrade gracefully under feature noise.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, the three contributions form a coherent pipeline: dataset trustworthiness is quantified, propagated through training and inference, and audited post-hoc at the model level. The use case shows that this pipeline can be deployed alongside an existing \ac{NN} without modifying its predictions, providing an interpretable and decomposable trust layer that supports downstream decision-making in a way that standard probabilistic outputs cannot.

\section{Summary \& Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This chapter applied the \acf{PaTAS} to a real-world use case in 5G network energy classification, demonstrating the end-to-end operation of the trust assessment pipeline on a dataset with heterogeneous hardware, automated labeling, and controlled data quality variation. We addressed four research questions.


\subsubsection*{Question 1: Trust Opinion Assignment}

\emph{How should trust opinions be assigned to real-world data?}

We introduced two trust quantification functions that map noise characteristics to subjective opinions: $\text{feature\_trust}(\sigma_{\text{rel}})$ for measurement noise (uncertainty-dominant, grounded in the noise-to-information ratio) and $\text{label\_trust}(p)$ for label-flipping errors (disbelief-dominant, reflecting the deterministic nature of flip-rate corruption). The two functions embed qualitatively different semantics into the opinion structure: feature noise increases vacuity without implying deception, while label noise encodes a known proportion of incorrect supervision with a fixed residual uncertainty $u_0 = 0.2$ reflecting the practical limits of flip-rate estimation.


\subsubsection*{Question 2: Trust Propagation Dynamics}

\emph{How do trust opinions propagate through the network during training?}

Input and label trust opinions propagate through the network via the discounting and fusion operators of \ac{SL}, accumulating evidence about parameter reliability across mini-batches. The forward-propagated opinion on the \ac{NN}'s predicted class provides a confidence signal grounded in both input quality and learned parameter trust. Crucially, this reasoning occurs in a parallel \ac{TNN} that does not affect the \ac{NN}'s gradients or predictions, preserving the original learning dynamics.


\subsubsection*{Question 3: \ac{PaTAS} as a Confidence Signal Under Data 
Quality Variation}

\emph{Does the trust signal produced by \ac{PaTAS} reliably indicate 
prediction correctness under data quality variation?}

Yes. Across all evaluated noise conditions, \ac{PaTAS} belief consistently discriminates between correct and incorrect \ac{NN} predictions. The signal is most stable under feature noise, where the discriminative gap $\Delta b$ remains in the range $[+0.228, +0.237]$ across $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \in \{0.10, 0.20, 0.30\}$, and degrades faster under label noise, where corruption of the supervision signal itself undermines parameter trust accumulation. Under combined noise the two effects compound, with the gap falling to $\Delta b = +0.043$ at the most severe condition. Crucially, the advantage over softmax filtering is largest precisely where it matters most: at low to moderate noise and coverage, where the noise-provenance information encoded in the trust opinion provides discriminative power that a scalar confidence score cannot replicate. This confirms that \ac{PaTAS} adds interpretable value beyond standard probabilistic outputs without modifying the underlying model.


\subsubsection*{Question 4: Per-Prediction Trust versus Global Calibration 
Trust}

\emph{How does \ac{PaTAS}-based per-prediction trust relate to a global calibration-trust assessment of the same model?}



The two assessments are complementary in granularity and sensitivity: \ac{PaTAS} varies per sample and is most discriminative under feature noise, while calibration trust summarizes the whole model and is most sensitive to label flipping. The first propagates input opinions forward through a parallel \ac{TNN} during inference; the second bins softmax predictions post-hoc. Despite operating through entirely different computational paths, both methods identify label noise as the more damaging corruption source and both degrade gracefully under feature noise. This coherence is not trivially guaranteed; it supports the broader claim that \ac{SL} provides a consistent language for trust quantification across different levels of the \ac{NN} pipeline, from individual predictions to global model auditing.

\subsection*{Contributions of This Chapter}

This use case validates the \ac{PaTAS} framework on three dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Methodological.} It demonstrates that the trust quantification, propagation, and calibration-trust components developed in \cref{chap:dataset-trust,chap:patas,chap:bb} compose into a coherent end-to-end pipeline on real data, with each component playing its intended role.
    \item \textbf{Empirical.} It provides quantitative evidence that \ac{PaTAS} opinions are discriminative across all evaluated noise conditions. Under feature noise, the discriminative gap $\Delta b$ remains stable between $+0.228$ and $+0.237$ across the evaluated range, while under label noise it degrades from $+0.140$ at $p = 0.05$ to $+0.041$ at $p = 0.30$. Used as a selective prediction filter, \ac{PaTAS} provides a consistent coverage-accuracy advantage over softmax filtering, with the largest gap observed under combined noise at $(\sigma_{\text{rel}}, p) = (0.10, 0.05)$: approximately 94\% versus 86\% accuracy at 20\% coverage. Global calibration trust further confirms that label flipping is the dominant driver of model-level miscalibration, while feature noise degrades accuracy without breaking confidence calibration.
    \item \textbf{Practical.} It shows that \ac{PaTAS} can be deployed alongside an existing \ac{NN} without modifying its training or predictions and sub-millisecond inference overhead across all evaluated batch sizes up to 1024.
\end{enumerate}

Together with the theoretical foundations established in earlier chapters, this evaluation completes the picture of trust assessment for \ac{NN} systems: from definition and quantification (\cref{chap:dataset-trust}), through propagation and aggregation (\cref{chap:patas}), to empirical validation in a production-relevant setting. The results demonstrate that trust reasoning, when grounded in \ac{SL} and calibrated to the actual data quality of the deployment domain, produces signals that are both interpretable and actionable.
