This chapter presents an optimized methodology for \acf{TNA-SL} which is often used to generate and evaluate expression in phase I and phase III of the Trust Assessment methodology presented in \cref{chap:Trust_method}. The chapter introduces foundational, algorithmic, and operator level improvements that address several limitations of the classical \ac{TNA-SL} methodology~\cite{4622580}. In particular, we propose a refined structural representation of trust networks based on \acf{PNPS}, derive optimized \ac{DSPG} synthesis criteria that preserve more information, develop an improved \ac{DSPG} analysis algorithm that reduces computational overhead, and introduce a new referral-edge path discounting operator ensuring consistent trust propagation over referral chains. The \ac{PNPS} structural concept was initially introduced through collaboration between Huawei colleagues and research partners in Luxembourg. Building on this definition, all theoretical developments presented in this chapter, including the formalization, analysis, and proofs of the proposed properties and algorithms, were carried out as part of this doctoral research project.

This chapter is based on two peer-reviewed publications. The structural and algorithmic optimization of \ac{DSPG} synthesis and analysis were published in \cite{10.1007/978-3-032-08887-1_4} and presented at the RuleML+RR 2025 conference. The referral-edge trust discounting operator and its theoretical properties were presented in \cite{10706345} at the FUSION 2024 conference. This chapter unifies these contributions into a coherent methodology for efficient and information-preserving trust inference.

\section{Introduction}
\acf{TNA-SL}, introduced by J{\o}sang~\cite{10.5555/1151699.1151710}, enables trust reasoning under uncertainty by propagating and aggregating subjective opinions along graph structures. In the methodology introduced in \cref{chap:Trust_method}, \ac{TNA-SL} is used on \acp{STN} to generate the expression in phase I. However, as discussed in \cref{sec:dspg}, a fundamental requirement to derive a trust opinion from an \ac{STN} is that the underlying trust network must be a \ac{DSPG}. This structural constraint ensures that trust propagation through discounting and fusion operators is well-defined and yields unambiguous results.

In practice, many real-world trust networks and computational graphs do not naturally satisfy the \ac{DSPG} property. In particular when considering a subnetwork from a \ac{NN} in which each layer contains a single neuron, modern architectures such as ResNet~\cite{he2015deepresiduallearningimage} and DenseNet~\cite{huang2018denselyconnectedconvolutionalnetworks} still exhibit skip connections that bypass intermediate layers or form overlapping dependency paths, as illustrated in \cref{fig:on_dspg_NN}. Such structures form \acp{DAG} that are not \ac{DSPG}. Similarly, real-world trust networks frequently exhibit complex connectivity patterns where trust relationships intersect across multiple paths. Consequently, a transformation step, referred to as \ac{DSPG} synthesis, is required to convert an arbitrary directed acyclic trust network into a valid \ac{DSPG} prior to performing trust inference.

The \ac{DSPG} synthesis approach proposed in the original work of J{\o}sang~\cite{10.5555/1151699.1151710} incrementally adds branches, using criteria to ensure that each new branch does not violate the \ac{DSPG} property of the graph. These criteria are overly restrictive conditions and often refuses edges considered structurally unsafe, even though adding these edges would not violate \ac{DSPG} compliance. This issue introduces two major limitations.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Information loss.} Meaningful trust relationships may be discarded, even when they do not fundamentally violate the \ac{DSPG} requirements.
    \item \textbf{Misaligned optimization objective.} Prior approaches implicitly assume that minimizing uncertainty should be the primary objective of synthesis. In practice, preserving as much information as possible should be the primary objective. \todo{Provide argument why}
\end{enumerate}

These observations reveal an inefficiency in existing \ac{DSPG} synthesis methods. The criteria are overly conservative, and the transformation objective is not aligned with information preservation.

At the same time, the \ac{DSPG} analysis phase performed after synthesis suffers from a computational inefficiency. The classical \ac{TNA-SL} reduction (\ac{DSPG} analysis) procedure repeatedly recomputes the \acp{NL} of the edges after each reduction. For large trust networks this leads to significant computational overhead and limits the scalability of the analysis procedure.

Finally, classical \ac{SL} trust discounting operators, namely the Two-Edge Path and Multi-Edge Path operators (\cref{table:TD_operators}), assume that the final edge of a path represents functional direct trust, which prevents their consistent application to referral-only paths. However, as illustrated in the \ac{IMA} use case (\cref{par:IMA_usecase}), certain network topologies require the discounting of such paths during the reduction process.

Therefore, the limitations of \ac{TNA-SL} are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A structural limitation caused by overly restrictive \ac{DSPG} synthesis criteria.
    \item A computational limitation arising from the repeated recomputation of edge \acp{NL} during \ac{DSPG} analysis, which increases the time complexity of trust inference.
    \item An operator-level limitation caused by incomplete trust discounting semantics for referral-only paths.
\end{itemize}
This chapter addresses these three limitations within a unified optimized methodology.

\subsection*{Unified Optimization Perspective}

We propose an improved \ac{TNA-SL} methodology that improves computational efficiency, preserves information, and ensures discounting consistency. This chapter presents five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Refined structural characterization via \ac{PNPS}.} We introduce the concept of \acf{PNPS}, which refines the classical definition of \acf{PPS}. This reformulation provides stronger structural guarantees and enables more efficient detection of \ac{DSPG} violations.

    \item \textbf{Optimized \ac{DSPG} synthesis criteria.} We derive a better synthesis criteria that admit a strictly larger class of edges while preserving \ac{DSPG} compliance. Unlike prior strategies, the proposed approach prioritizes maximal information retention during synthesis.

    \item \textbf{Optimized \ac{DSPG} synthesis algorithm.}
    Building on the revised synthesis criteria, we propose an algorithm that incrementally constructs a \ac{DSPG} while maximizing information retention. The algorithm allows efficient verification of synthesis conditions.

    \item \textbf{Optimized \ac{DSPG} analysis algorithm.}
    By operating on \ac{PNPS} structures rather than \ac{PPS}, we propose an algorithm that ensures structural isolation during reductions and streamlines the computation of \acp{NL}, thereby reducing unnecessary recomputations. We formally establish the corresponding \ac{NL} preservation properties.
    
    \item \textbf{Referral-edge path discounting operator.} To resolve inconsistencies in referral-only chains, we introduce a novel trust discount operator that extends \ac{SL} discounting semantics to paths composed entirely of referral edges. The proposed operator is compatible with existing Two-Edge and Multi-Edge operators and preserves expected trust attenuation properties across increasing path lengths.
\end{enumerate}
Taken together, these contributions make \ac{TNA-SL} more general, more information-preserving, and more computationally efficient. As a result, the methodology becomes better suited for complex systems such as \ac{NN} systems, where trust must be propagated across heterogeneous and potentially unreliable information sources.

The remainder of this chapter formalizes the proposed framework. \cref{sec:pnps} introduces the \ac{PNPS} concept and the associated structural reformulation.  \cref{sec:optimized_criteria} presents the improved \ac{DSPG} synthesis criteria, while \cref{sec:optimized_algorithm} introduces the algorithm that implements DSPG synthesis based on these criteria. \cref{sec:optimized_analysis} details the optimized analysis algorithm and establishes its correctness properties. \cref{sec:referral_path_operator} introduces the new referral-edge discount operator and analyzes its compatibility with classical Subjective Logic operators. \cref{sec:eval_tnasl} presents the evaluation. Finally, \cref{sec:summary_tnsl} concludes the chapter.

\section{Parallel Non-Intersecting Path Subnetworks}
\label{sec:pnps}

To address the limitations of \ac{DSPG} synthesis and analysis procedures identified in the introduction, we refine the notion of \ac{PPS} originally used in \ac{DSPG} analysis. While \ac{PPS} structures are sufficient for defining the reduction order in classical \ac{DSPG} analysis, they are not strong enough to guarantee isolation during analysis and \acp{NL} updates.

For example, consider the graph shown in \cref{fig:pps_before,fig:pps_after}. If the \ac{PPS} between nodes \(A\) and \(C\) is reduced into a single edge \(A\to C\), the \ac{NL} of this newly created edge becomes \(0\), while the \acp{NL} of the remaining edges decrease by one. This global update illustrates that reductions based on \ac{PPS} structures do not preserve local isolation during the reduction process, since the transformation affects the \acp{NL} of edges outside the reduced subnetwork.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a stricter structural construct called the \acf{PNPS}. This refinement plays a central role in enabling both a clearer formalization of \ac{DSPG} structures and stable \acp{NL} computation after \ac{PNPS} reduction into a single edge.

\begin{definition}[\aclu{PNPS}]
\label{def:pnps}
Let $(A,B)$ be a \ac{PPS} (\cref{def:pps}). It is called a \ac{PNPS} if and only if there exist at least two non-intersecting directed paths from the source $A$ to the sink $B$.
\end{definition}
The key difference between \ac{PPS} and \ac{PNPS} lies in the non-intersection requirement. This distinction is illustrated in \cref{fig:pps_before,fig:pps_after,fig:pnps_before_1,fig:pnps_after_1}. For instance, although $(A,G)$ qualifies as a \ac{PPS}, it is not a \ac{PNPS} because we cannot find two non-intersecting paths between $A$ and $G$. On the other hand, if we add a path from $A$ to $G$ via a new node $H$, then $(A,G)$ becomes a \ac{PNPS} (\cref{fig:pnps_before_2,fig:pnps_after_2}). \todo{explain also once more how the nesting levels are calculated and why they are different in A and C.}

% \subsection{Structural Characterization of Non-DSPG}

The introduction of \ac{PNPS} enables a simpler characterization of the \ac{DSPG} property. In particular, the following proposition provides a way for detecting graphs that do not satisfy the \ac{DSPG} property.
\begin{proposition}[\ac{DSPG} Constraint]
\label{prop:dspg}
A \ac{DAG} is not a \ac{DSPG} if and only if there exists a \ac{PNPS} containing an intermediate node that has either
\begin{itemize}
    \item an outgoing edge to a node outside the \ac{PNPS}, or
    \item an incoming edge from a node outside the \ac{PNPS}.
\end{itemize}

\end{proposition}

This proposition provides an operational detection rule: a \ac{PNPS} that satisfies this property cannot be safely reduced to a single edge. By focusing exclusively on \ac{PNPS} structures, we obtain stronger structural guarantees and simpler verification conditions.

As discussed in \cref{sec:dspg}, the original \ac{SL} work defines a set of criteria to be used when synthesizing a \ac{DSPG} from a non-\ac{DSPG} graph. Using the \ac{PNPS} definition introduced earlier, we reformulate these criteria in structural terms.

\begin{corollary}[\ac{PNPS}-Based \ac{DSPG} Criteria]
\label{cor:pnps-criteria}
Let $A$ and $B$ be two nodes of the current graph, which is assumed to be a \ac{DSPG}. Adding a branch from $A$ to $B$ preserves the \ac{DSPG} property if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Criterion 1}: There exists at least one path from $A$ to $B$ in the current graph.
    \item \textbf{Criterion 2}: If $A$ is an intermediate node of a \ac{PNPS} $P$, then $B$ must also be an intermediate node of $P$, and vice versa.
\end{itemize}
\end{corollary}

This formulation replaces \ac{NNL} arithmetic conditions with a structural containment rule. It directly captures part of the structural isolation constraint established in \cref{prop:dspg}.

Before establishing the equivalence between the original \ac{NNL}-based criteria and the structural \ac{PNPS}-based formulation, we introduce an alternative characterization of the \ac{NNL}. While the classical definition of \ac{NNL} is expressed as a counting measure over \acp{PPS}, the structural interpretation of \ac{DSPG} graphs suggests a formulation in terms of the nesting depth of \acp{PNPS}. The following theorem shows that the \aclu{NL} of a node can equivalently be expressed as the maximum \aclu{NL} of the \acp{PNPS} in which the node appears as an intermediate node. This result provides the bridge between the arithmetic \ac{NNL} formulation and the rules used in the \ac{PNPS}-based criteria. Throughout the remainder of this work, we denote by $\mathcal{P}_G$ the set of all \acp{PNPS} of a graph $G$.

\begin{theorem}[\ac{PNPS}-Based Characterization of \ac{NNL}]
\label{thm:pnps-nnl}
Let $G$ be a \ac{DSPG} and let $v$ be a node of $G$. Then
\begin{align}
\label{eq:pnps-nnl}
\mathrm{NNL}_G(v) = 
\max\left\{
\mathrm{PNL}_G(P)
\;\middle|\;
P\in\mathcal{P}_G,\;
v \text{ is an intermediate node of } P
\right\}, 
\end{align}

where
\[
\mathrm{PNL}_G(P)
=
\min\left\{
\mathrm{NL}_G(e)
\;\middle|\;
e \text{ is an edge of } P
\right\},
\]
with the convention that the maximum over the empty set is $0$.
\end{theorem}
Intuitively, in a \ac{DSPG} the \acp{PNPS} for which a node is an intermediate node are nested. The \ac{NNL} of the node therefore corresponds to the deepest \ac{PNPS}.

\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\mathrm{NNL}_G(v)=
\left|
\left\{
P\in\mathcal{P}_G
\;\middle|\;
v \text{ is an intermediate node of } P
\right\}
\right|.
\]

Thus, $\mathrm{NNL}_G(v)$ counts the number of subnetworks in which $v$ appears as an intermediate node. In classical \ac{DSPG} analysis these subnetworks correspond to \acp{PPS}, whereas in the present formulation we reason over the equivalent \ac{PNPS} structures.

Let
\[
\mathcal{P}_G(v)=
\left\{
P\in\mathcal{P}_G
\;\middle|\;
v \text{ is an intermediate node of } P
\right\}
\]
denote the set of \acp{PNPS} in which $v$ appears as an intermediate node.

If $\mathcal{P}_G(v)=\emptyset$, then by definition $\mathrm{NNL}_G(v)=0$.
The right-hand side of \cref{eq:pnps-nnl} is also the maximum over the empty set,
which by convention equals $0$. Hence the equality holds.

Assume now that $\mathcal{P}_G(v)\neq\emptyset$.
Because $G$ is a \ac{DSPG}, the \acp{PNPS} containing $v$ as an intermediate node
must be nested. Indeed, if two such subnetworks $P_1$ and $P_2$ were not nested,
it would be possible to exit $P_1$ toward $P_2$ (or vice versa) without first
reaching the target of $P_1$, which contradicts the \ac{DSPG} structural constraint
(\cref{prop:dspg}). Therefore the elements of $\mathcal{P}_G(v)$ form a chain
under inclusion.

Let
\[
P^\star \in \mathcal{P}_G(v)
\]
be the most nested \ac{PNPS} in this chain. Since $P^\star$ is contained in
all other subnetworks of $\mathcal{P}_G(v)$, every edge of $P^\star$ belongs
to every $P \in \mathcal{P}_G(v)$. Hence, for any edge $e \in P^\star$,
\[
e \in P^\star \Rightarrow e \in P, \quad \forall P\in\mathcal{P}_G(v).
\]

Therefore each such edge belongs to at least $|\mathcal{P}_G(v)|$ subnetworks,
which implies
\[
\mathrm{NL}_G(e)\ge |\mathcal{P}_G(v)|.
\]

By definition of $\mathrm{PNL}$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star)
    =
    \min\left\{
    \mathrm{NL}_G(e)
    \;\middle|\;
    e \text{ is an edge of } P^\star
    \right\},
\end{equation}
and thus
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:proof_nnl_1}
    \mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star)\ge |\mathcal{P}_G(v)|
\end{equation}

Conversely, consider an edge $e_v\in P^\star$ whose sink is $v$.
Let $P\in\mathcal{P}_G$ be a \ac{PNPS} such that $e_v\in P$.
Since $P^\star$ is the most nested \ac{PNPS} containing $v$, it follows from the
\ac{DSPG} structural constraint (\cref{prop:dspg}) that
\[
e_v\in P \;\Rightarrow\; v \text{ is an intermediate node of } P \;\Rightarrow\; P\in\mathcal{P}_G(v) .
\]

Indeed, $v$ cannot be the target of $P$. Otherwise, it would be possible to exit $P$ and enter $P^\star$ through $v$ without first reaching the source of $P^\star$, which contradicts the \ac{DSPG} structural constraint.

Hence $v$ must be an intermediate node of $P$, which implies $P\in\mathcal{P}_G(v)$.

Therefore
\[
\left|
\left\{
P\in\mathcal{P}_G
\;\middle|\;
e_v\in P
\right\}
\right|
\le
|\mathcal{P}_G(v)|.
\]

Since $\mathrm{NL}_G(e_v)$ counts exactly the left hand side quantity, we obtain
\[
\mathrm{NL}_G(e_v)\le |\mathcal{P}_G(v)|.
\]

Because $\mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star)$ is the minimum $\mathrm{NL}$ among the edges
of $P^\star$ (and $e_v \in P^\star$), it follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:proof_nnl_2}
    \mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star)\le |\mathcal{P}_G(v)|.
\end{equation}



Combining the two inequalities yields \cref{eq:proof_nnl_1,eq:proof_nnl_2}
\[
\mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star)=|\mathcal{P}_G(v)|.
\]
Finally, 
\begin{align}
\mathrm{NNL}_G(v) &= |\mathcal{P}_G(v)| \notag \\
&= \mathrm{PNL}_G(P^\star) \notag \\
&= \max\left\{
\mathrm{PNL}_G(P)
\;\middle|\;
P\in\mathcal{P}_G,\; 
v \text{ is an intermediate node of } P
\right\}. \notag
\end{align}

This proves the claim.
\end{proof}
Having established that the \ac{NNL} of a node corresponds to the nesting depth  of its deepest enclosing \ac{PNPS}, we are now in a position to compare the \ac{PNPS}-based criteria of \cref{cor:pnps-criteria} with J{\o}sang's original \ac{NNL}-arithmetic formulation. The following lemma provides the logical infrastructure needed for that equivalence proof.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma}
Let $H$, $I$, $J$, and $K$ be logical statements. Suppose we want to prove the equivalence:
\[
(H \wedge I) \Leftrightarrow (J \Leftrightarrow K).
\]
We can analyze this equivalence using contrapositive reasoning as follows.

First, recall that:
\[
\neg(H \wedge I) = \neg H \vee \neg I
\]
and
\[
\neg(J \Leftrightarrow K) = \neg\left((J \Rightarrow K) \wedge (K \Rightarrow J)\right) = (J \wedge \neg K) \vee (K \wedge \neg J).
\]

Therefore, proving the equivalence:
\(
(H \wedge I) \Leftrightarrow (J \Leftrightarrow K)
\)
is logically equivalent to proving:
\[
(J \wedge \neg K) \vee (K \wedge \neg J) \Leftrightarrow \neg H \vee \neg I.
\]

In particular, if we only want to prove a one-way implication:
\(
(H \wedge I) \Rightarrow (J \Rightarrow K),
\)
we can equivalently prove the contrapositive:
\[
(J \wedge \neg K) \Rightarrow (\neg H \vee \neg I).
\]
\end{lemma}

\noindent We apply this lemma in the proofs of \cref{thm:criteria-equivalence,thm:optimized-sound}.

With this tool in hand, we can now formally establish that the \ac{PNPS}-based criteria and the original arithmetic \ac{NNL}-based criteria are two expressions of the same underlying constraint.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalence of Criteria]
\label{thm:criteria-equivalence}
When the current graph is \ac{DSPG} (\cref{def:dspg_original_criteria}), J{\o}sang's original criteria are logically equivalent to the \ac{PNPS}-based \ac{DSPG} criteria of \cref{cor:pnps-criteria}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We apply \cref{lemma} with the following instantiation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $J \Leftrightarrow K$: $A$ is an intermediate node of a \ac{PNPS} $P$ 
    if and only if $B$ is an intermediate node of $P$.
    \item $H$: $\mathrm{NNL}(A) = \mathrm{NNL}(B)$.
    \item $I$: every intermediate node $C$ between $A$ and $B$ satisfies 
    $\mathrm{NNL}(C) \geq \mathrm{NNL}(A)$.
\end{itemize}
We then prove each direction.

($(J \wedge \neg K) \Rightarrow (\neg H \vee \neg I)$)

If $A$ is an intermediate node of a PNPS $P$ and $B$ is not. This means that the sink $C$ of $P$ is before $B$ in a path from $A$ to $B$. Therefore, $NNL(C)<NNL(A)$. 

($(K \wedge \neg J) \Rightarrow (\neg H \vee \neg I)$) 

The same logic as before applies when $B$ is an intermediate node of a PNPS $P$ and $A$ is not.
    
($\neg H \Rightarrow (J \wedge \neg K) \vee (K \wedge \neg J)  $)

If $NNL(A) \neq NNL(B)$ then by definition we can find a PNPS in which one node ($A$ or $B$) is an intermediate node, but the other not. If instead some intermediate node $C$ satisfies $\mathrm{NNL}(C) < \mathrm{NNL}(A)$, then the sink of the most nested \ac{PNPS} containing $A$ lies at or before $C$ on path from $A$ to $B$, and therefore before $B$, so $B$ is not an intermediate node of that \ac{PNPS}.
\end{proof}

Although equivalent to the original formulation, the \ac{PNPS}-based perspective reveals that strict equality of \acp{NNL} is not strictly necessary. Correctness is preserved as long as no  \ac{PNPS} isolation constraint is violated. We therefore introduce optimized criteria.

\begin{definition}[Optimized \ac{DSPG} Synthesis Criteria]
\label{def:optimized-criteria}
A branch from $A \to B$ can be safely added if and only if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Criterion 1:} There exists a path from $A$ to $B$.
    \item \textbf{Criterion 2} (Containment conditions): 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $A$ is an intermediate node of a  \ac{PNPS} $P$, then $B$ must lie inside $P$.
    \item If $B$ is an intermediate node of a  \ac{PNPS} $P$, then $A$ must lie inside $P$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The difference compared to the original criteria is that we allow $A$ or $B$ to be the source or target of the same \ac{PNPS}, not necessarily intermediate nodes. This strictly enlarges the admissible class of branches.

\begin{theorem}[Soundness and Completeness]
\label{thm:optimized-sound}
Let $A \rightarrow B$ be a candidate branch.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item If the branch satisfies the optimized criteria, the resulting graph remains \ac{DSPG}. \label{thm:firstpart}
    \item If adding the branch does not violate the \ac{DSPG} property, then the branch satisfies the optimized criteria. \label{thm:secondpart}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This proof is based on the logical structure outlined in \cref{lemma}

For \ref{thm:firstpart}: We know that if $A$ is an intermediate node of a PNPS $p$ then $B$ is an intermediate node of $p$ and if $B$ is an intermediate node of a PNPS $p$ then $A$ is an intermediate node of $p$ implies that the \ac{DSPG} property not broken. What we need to show in this proof is that adding an edge from the source to an intermediate node or from an intermediate node to the sink inside a PNPS $p$ with maximum \ac{NL} does not affect the \ac{DSPG} property of the graph. We restrict the proof to the \ac{PNPS} of maximum \ac{NL} without loss of generality since it is the same as doing the proof for any \ac{PNPS}. In fact, what we want to know is whether we can still reduce a \ac{PNPS} to a single edge using the \ac{DSPG} analysis algorithm depicted in \cref{fig:algsynth2}. 
\\
During the reduction process, the reduction will be done for this \ac{PNPS} when the \ac{PNPS} will be the most nested (the PNPS with maximum \ac{NL}).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item add an edge from the source $A$ to an intermediate node $C$:
        Assuming that the current \ac{PNPS} has max \ac{NL}, adding this branch does not break the \ac{DSPG} property because after adding this branch the max \ac{NL} will be increased by one. Then the new PNPS with maximum \ac{NL} will be the new one created  $[A,C]$. This new PNPS will be like \cref{fig:pr} because it is clear that there is only one path from $A$ to $C$ otherwise $A$ and $C$ would have been the source and sink of a PNPS with \ac{NL} greater than \ac{NL} of $p$.
    
        \item add an edge from an intermediate node $C$ to the target $B$: Same logic as before. We can just reverse the graph and use the same proof.
    \end{itemize}

    
For \ref{thm:secondpart}: we show that adding $A \to B$ does not break the \ac{DSPG} property. Assuming that the first criterion of criteria defined in \cref{cor:pnps-criteria} is fulfilled, then \cref{prop:dspg} implies that $A$ to $B$ fulfilled Criterion 2 of the optimized Criteria for \ac{DSPG} Synthesis.
\end{proof}

\section{Optimized \acs{DSPG} Synthesis Algorithm}
\label{sec:optimized_algorithm}

In previous \cref{sec:optimized_criteria}, we established necessary and sufficient conditions under which a branch can be added to a \ac{DSPG} without breaking its \ac{DSPG} property. We now present a constructive algorithm that implements these criteria and synthesizes a \ac{DSPG} from an arbitrary \ac{DAG}. The algorithm incrementally builds a \ac{DSPG} by selecting admissible branches according to the optimized criteria while maintaining information about \ac{PNPS} membership.

% \subsection{Algorithm Overview}

Let $G = (V,E)$ be a \ac{DAG} that may not satisfy the \ac{DSPG} property. The synthesis procedure constructs a subgraph $G' \subseteq G$ that is \ac{DSPG}-compliant. The key idea is based on previous work~\cite{4622580}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Enumerate all source-to-sink paths in $G$.
    \item Initialize $G'$ with one valid path.
    \item Iteratively attempt to add remaining branches.
    \item Accept a branch if and only if it satisfies the optimized criteria.
\end{enumerate}

To efficiently evaluate our conditions, we maintain a mapping
\[
nodesToPNPS : V \rightarrow \{\text{highest nested  \ac{PNPS} containing the node}\}.
\]
This maps a node of the graph to its highest nested  \ac{PNPS}. This mapping allows constant-time verification of \ac{PNPS} containment constraints.

% \subsection{Algorithm Description}

\cref{alg:dspg-synth} presents the synthesis procedure.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Optimized \ac{DSPG} Synthesis}
\label{alg:dspg-synth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require \ac{DAG} $G = (V,E)$
\Ensure \ac{DSPG} subgraph $G' \subseteq G$
\State $paths \gets$ GetAllPaths($G$)
\State Sort $paths$ by uncertainty (after discounting the path) or length
\State $G' \gets$ $paths.pop()$ \Comment{Initialize with first path}
\State $nodesToPNPS \gets \emptyset$
\While{$paths \neq \emptyset$}
    \State $path \gets paths.pop()$
    \State $branch \gets$ first branch of $path$ not in $G'$
    \State $(A,B) \gets (source(branch), target(branch))$
    \State $check \gets$ DSPGChecker($nodesToPNPS$, $A$, $B$)
    \If{$check.bool = \text{True}$}
        \State $G'.addEdges(branch)$

        \State UpdatePNPS($nodesToPNPS$, $check.intermediateNodes$,$branch.intermediateNodes$, $(A,B)$)

    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{\ac{DSPG}Checker}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Dictionary $\textit{nodesToPNPS}$, source node $src$, target node $target$
\If{there is a path from $src$ to $target$}
    \State $pathNodes \gets$ list of all intermediate nodes in this path
\Else
    \State \Return $\{bool: False\}$
\EndIf

\If{$nodesToPNPS[src] \neq None$}
    \If{$target \notin nodesToPNPS[src]$}
        \State \Return $\{bool: False\}$
    \EndIf
\EndIf

\If{$nodesToPNPS[target] \neq None$}
    \If{$src \notin nodesToPNPS[target]$}
        \State \Return $\{bool: False\}$
    \EndIf
\EndIf

\State \Return $\{bool: True,\; intermediateNodes: pathNodes\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{UpdatePNPS}
\label{alg:update-pnps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $nodesToPNPS$ : current map from node $\rightarrow$ PNPS
\Require branch $BranchListOfIntermediateNodes$
\Require checker $CheckerListOfIntermediateNodes$
\Require $(nodeSource, nodeTarget)$ : candidate PNPS

\Ensure $nodesToPNPS$ maps each node to the **highest nested PNPS** it is part of

\For{$v \in$ $CheckerListOfIntermediateNodes$}
\State $nodesToPNPS[v] \gets smallerPNPS((nodeSource, nodeTarget),\ nodesToPNPS[v])$
\EndFor

\For{v $\in$ $BranchListOfIntermediateNodes$}
\State $nodesToPNPS[v] \gets moreNestedPNPS((nodeSource, nodeTarget),\ nodesToPNPS[v])$
\EndFor

\State \Return $nodesToPNPS$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The procedures \texttt{DSPGChecker} and \texttt{UpdatePNPS} support the incremental synthesis process. The function \texttt{DSPGChecker} verifies whether a candidate branch $(src,target)$ satisfies the optimized synthesis criteria. It first checks the existence of a path from $src$ to $target$, and then ensures that the containment conditions imposed by \cref{def:optimized-criteria} are respected using the current \texttt{nodesToPNPS} mapping. If the branch is admissible, the procedure returns the intermediate nodes along the corresponding path. 

The procedure \texttt{UpdatePNPS} then updates the mapping \texttt{nodesToPNPS} to maintain the invariant that each node is associated with the most deeply nested \ac{PNPS} in which it appears as an intermediate node. When a new branch creates or extends a \ac{PNPS} between two nodes, the intermediate nodes of the existing path and those of the new branch are examined. Nodes that become part of a more deeply nested \ac{PNPS} are updated accordingly, while nodes already belonging to a deeper \ac{PNPS} remain unchanged.  This ensures that the mapping always reflects the highest nested structural context of each node.


Following we show different properties of the \cref{alg:dspg-synth}. The correctness of the algorithm relies on the invariant that $nodesToPNPS[v]$ always stores the most deeply nested  \ac{PNPS} in which node $v$ appears as an intermediate node.
\begin{theorem}
Let $a$ and $b$ be two nodes, and assume that we have a mapping 
$nodesToPNPS$ which maps a node to the highest nested PNPS it is part of.
Then we have

\begin{align}
    nodesToPNPS[a] = None \; \text{ or }\; b \in nodesToPNPS[a]
\iff \notag \\
\text{if node } a \text{ is an intermediate node of a PNPS } p
\text{ then } b \text{ is inside } p . \notag 
\end{align}


\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}

($\Rightarrow$)
If $nodesToPNPS[a] = None$, then $a$ is not part of any PNPS.
Therefore the second statement is trivially true.

If $b \in nodesToPNPS[a]$, then $b$ belongs to the most nested PNPS
containing $a$. Since every PNPS containing $a$ also contains
$nodesToPNPS[a]$, it follows that $b$ is part of any PNPS that
contains $a$.

($\Leftarrow$)
If node $a$ is an intermediate node of a PNPS $P$ and $b$ is inside $P$,
then $b$ belongs to every PNPS of which $a$ is an intermediate node.
Since $nodesToPNPS[a]$ represents the most nested PNPS containing $a$,
it follows that $b \in nodesToPNPS[a]$.

\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
The update of $nodesToPNPS$ in \cref{alg:update-pnps} ensures that $nodesToPNPS$ maps each node to the highest nested PNPS it is part of.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}

We want to prove that if before the update of $nodesToPNPS$ the mapping
associates each node with the highest nested PNPS it belongs to,
then after the update this property still holds.

When adding a new branch from $A \to B$, two situations may occur.

\textbf{Case 1:} The subnetwork from $A$ to $B$ was already a PNPS.
In this case, the update only assigns values to the newly added nodes,
while the existing nodes already have the correct mapping.

\textbf{Case 2:} The subnetwork from $A$ to $B$ forms a new PNPS.
Two subcases arise.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{No PNPS between $A$ and $B$.}  
A new PNPS is created. The intermediate nodes of this PNPS are exactly
the nodes on the branch being added and the intermediate nodes on the
path from $A$ to $B$ before adding the branch. These nodes are therefore
updated to map to this newly created PNPS.

\item \textbf{Existing PNPS between $A$ and $B$.}  
Some intermediate nodes on the path from $A$ to $B$ may already belong
to a more nested PNPS. For these nodes, $nodesToPNPS$ is not updated
because they already map to a more nested PNPS. The nodes that are not
part of a more nested PNPS are updated accordingly. This is valid
because every path from $A$ to $B$ before adding the new branch
contains these nodes, meaning they are not intermediate nodes of any
PNPS between $A$ and $B$.

\end{itemize}

Therefore, after the update, each node still maps to the highest nested
PNPS it belongs to.

\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Algorithm Soundness]
\label{thm:alg-sound}
At every iteration, $G'$ remains \ac{DSPG}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The algorithm adds a branch only if it satisfies the optimized synthesis criteria of \cref{thm:optimized-sound}. By soundness of those criteria, the addition preserves the \ac{DSPG} property. Since the initial path is trivially \ac{DSPG} and each step preserves the invariant, the resulting graph remains \ac{DSPG}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Algorithm Completeness]
\label{thm:alg-complete}
If a branch can be added without violating the \ac{DSPG} property, the algorithm will accept it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By completeness of the optimized criteria in \cref{thm:optimized-sound}, any branch that preserves \ac{DSPG} must satisfy the structural containment conditions. The \texttt{DSPGChecker} verifies exactly these conditions. Therefore admissible branches are not rejected.
\end{proof}

% \subsection{Termination}

\begin{theorem}[Termination]
\label{thm:termination}
The algorithm terminates after a finite number of iterations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The number of paths in a finite \ac{DAG} is finite. At each iteration, one path is removed from the list and processed. No path is reinserted. Therefore the algorithm terminates.
\end{proof}

% \subsection{Complexity Considerations}

Let $P$ denote the number of source-to-sink paths in $G$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Path enumeration is exponential in the worst case.
    \item Each branch verification is constant time assuming $nodesToPNPS$ is maintained efficiently.
    \item  \ac{PNPS} updates are linear in the size of the branch.
\end{itemize}

Although path enumeration may be expensive in theory, in trust networks the graph structure is typically sparse and limited in depth. Moreover, synthesis is performed once prior to trust inference, making preprocessing cost acceptable.

\section{Optimized \acs{DSPG} Analysis and \acs{PNPS}-Based Reduction}
\label{sec:optimized_analysis}
Once a \ac{DSPG} has been synthesized, trust inference proceeds through reduction. Classical \ac{DSPG} analysis recursively reduces the graph by replacing the most nested \acp{PPS} with a single edge whose subjective opinion corresponds to the expression obtained by combining the opinions along the paths of the \ac{PPS}. This process continues until only one edge remains between the global source and the global sink.

In the classical formulation, reduction relies on two elementary operations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Series reduction, which reduces two consecutive edges incident to a node of degree two, then we compute the opinion on the new edge using trust discounting (\cref{subsec:TD_operators}).
    \item Parallel reduction, which reduces parallel edges into a single edge, then we compute the opinion on the new edge using belief fusion (\cref{sec:fusion}).
\end{enumerate}

However, in the \ac{PPS}-based framework these reductions are not structurally isolated. Replacing a \ac{PPS} with a single edge may alter the structure of other \acp{PPS} in the graph. For example, in \cref{fig:pnps}, reducing the \ac{PPS} \(A \to C\) modifies the graph and the subnetwork \(A \to G\) is no longer a \ac{PPS}. As a consequence, reductions can induce changes across the graph, making the evolution of \acp{NL} difficult to track precisely. In this section, we show that replacing \ac{PPS} with \ac{PNPS} yields a localized reduction process. We formalize the corresponding reduction procedure and analyze the resulting \aclu{NL} updates.

\begin{definition}[ \ac{PNPS} Reduction]
Let $P$ be a  \ac{PNPS} with source $A$ and target $B$. A  \ac{PNPS} reduction replaces the entire subnetwork $P$ with a single edge $(A,B)$ whose subjective opinion equals the fusion of the discounted opinions along the paths in \(P\).
\end{definition}

The proposed optimized analysis algorithm (illustrated in \cref{fig:algsynth2}) follows the same process as the classical one, but relies on PNPS reduction to ensure NL updates proceed in a controlled manner. The key property that makes this possible is formalized in the following theorem, which establishes both the isolation of each reduction step and the predictable evolution of \acp{NL}.

\begin{theorem}[\ac{PNPS} Reduction Preservation and \acp{NL} Update]
\label{thm:pnps-reduction}
Let $G$ be a \ac{DSPG}, and let $\text{pnps}_1$ be a \ac{PNPS} in $G$ with the maximum \ac{NL}. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{(Isolated Transformation)} Transforming $\text{pnps}_1$ into a single edge does not affect the \ac{NL} of any other PNPS in the graph.
    \item \textbf{(\ac{NL} Update)} The edge that replaces $\text{pnps}_1$ has a \aclu{NL} equal to $\text{NL}(\text{pnps}_1) - 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
The theorem formalizes two essential properties. First, reduction is isolated, meaning that the transformation of a maximally nested \ac{PNPS} does not alter the remaining \acp{PNPS}. Second, \acp{NL} decrease in a controlled manner.

The proof of \cref{thm:pnps-reduction} relies on relationships between nested \acp{PNPS}. Before providing the proof, we need to introduce several elementary results that characterize the structural relationships between nested PNPSs. These results are necessary to justify both the isolation property and the \ac{NL} update described above.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thrm:anlsys}
Consider a \ac{DSPG} and two PNPSs, $pnps_1$ and $pnps_2$, such that $NL(pnps_1) > NL(pnps_2)$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{thm:p1} If a node $s$ is the common source of both $pnps_1$ and $pnps_2$, then $s$ must have at least one outgoing edge that is in $pnps_2$ but not in $pnps_1$.
    \item \label{thm:p2} If a node $t$ is the common target of both $pnps_1$ and $pnps_2$, then $t$ must have at least one incoming edge that is in $pnps_2$ but not in $pnps_1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\label{prf:anlsys1}
For part~\ref{thm:p1} of the theorem.
If $NL(pnps_1) > NL(pnps_2)$, then $pnps_1$ and $pnps_2$ don't have the same target (since they already have the same source). Thus:
\begin{itemize}
    \item  either $pnps_1$ is inside $pnps_2$: If $s$ does not have at least one outgoing edge to a node which is inside $pnps_2$ and outside $pnps_1$ it means that all paths from $s$ to target of $pnps_2$ will be through $pnps_1$ since from an intermediate node of $pnps_1$ we cannot go outside $pnps_1$ without reaching the target of $pnps_1$. The consequence is that we will not be able to find two non-intersecting paths therefore the graph is not \ac{DSPG};
    
    \item or $pnps_1$ is not inside $pnps_2$ and $Nodes(pnps_1) \cap Nodes(pnps_1) \neq \{s\}$: In this case the graph cannot be a \ac{DSPG} because it means that $pnps_1$ has an intermediate node $n_1$ inside $pnps_2$ (which are not $s$) and a node $n_2$  outside $pnps_2$. $n_2$ is an intermediate node of $pnps_2$ (otherwise it’s the target node of $pnps_2$, and in this case it impossible to have $NL(pnps_1)>NL(pnps_2 )$). Therefore, from $n_1$ (intermediate node of $pnps_2$) we can reach $n_2$ (outside $pnps_2$)  without visiting the target of $pnps_2$;
    
    \item or $pnps_1$ is not inside $pnps_2$ and $Nodes(pnps_1) \cap Nodes(pnps_1) = \{s\}$: In this case $s$ has at least one outgoing edge to a node which is inside $pnps_2$ and outside $pnps_1$.
\end{itemize}

The part~\ref{thm:p2} of the theorem is the dual case of part~\ref{thm:p1}. Reverse the graph and apply the same reasoning.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{corol:synth}
Considering a \ac{DSPG}, if a PNPS $pnps_1$ is nested in another PNPS $pnps_2$ and $pnps_1 \neq pnps_2$, then $pnps_2$ has at least one edge which is not in $pnps_1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    If a PNPS $pnps_1$ is nested in another PNPS $pnps_2$, we can distinguish between two cases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $pnps_1$ and $pnps_2$ do not share the same source and also do not share the same target nodes: if they don't have the same source then the path from the source of $pnps_2$ to the source of $pnps_1$ contains edges outside $pnps_1$. Same reasoning  if they don't have the same target.
    \item $pnps_1$ shares the source or the target (or both) with $pnps_2$: application of Theorems \cref{thrm:anlsys}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}
We now return to the proof of \cref{thm:pnps-reduction} stated above. 
\begin{proof}
    When reducing a PNPS with the maximum \ac{NL} (denoted as $pnps_1$), two structural cases arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $pnps_1$ is a non nested PNPS, or
    \item $pnps_1$  is nested within another PNPS (denoted as $pnps_2$).
\end{enumerate}
In case (1), $pnps_1$ has a \ac{NL} equal to 1. After its reduction, all other PNPSs remains the same and the resulting edge has a \ac{NL} of 0. In the case (2), since at least one edge --- denoted as $e$ --- in $pnps_2$ is not part of $pnps_1$ (\cref{corol:synth}), $NL(e)$ will remain unchanged and we select it such that its \ac{NL} equals that of $pnps_2$ (this follows from \cref{thrm:anlsys} with $pnps_1$ \ac{NL} equals $pnps_2$ \ac{NL} plus 1. Such a PNPS $pnps_2$ exists, as the \ac{NL} increments one by one). As a result, the \ac{NL} of $pnps_1$ is preserved, since the new edge has \ac{NL} reduced by one which is still greater than or equal to that of $pnps_2$.
\end{proof}

An immediate consequence of \cref{thm:pnps-reduction} is that the algorithm always progresses from the most nested \ac{PNPS} outward. At each step, the remaining PNPSs retain their nesting structure, ensuring that the next iteration is well-defined and that the process terminates in a finite number of steps.

In summary Replacing \ac{PPS} with \ac{PNPS} produces three fundamental improvements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stronger isolation guarantees.}  \ac{PNPS} ensures that intermediate nodes cannot connect to external structures without violating \ac{DSPG}.
    \item \textbf{Simplified detection of non-\ac{DSPG} patterns.} Structural violations become local and verifiable through \cref{prop:dspg}.
    \item \textbf{Stable nesting updates.} \cref{thm:pnps-reduction} guarantees predictable nesting-level evolution during reduction.
\end{enumerate}
These properties form the backbone of the optimized synthesis and analysis methodology presented in this chapter. We now turn to the operator-level limitation identified previously.








\section{Trust Discounting for Referral Paths}
\label{sec:referral_path_operator}

In \cref{par:IMA_usecase}, we identified a limitation of existing trust discounting operators: neither $\otimes_{TE}$ nor $\otimes_{ME}$\todo{If you use operators or acronyms after longer times, quickly recap what they are.} supports discounting restricted to referral edges only. Specifically, when a path consists entirely of referral edges, as may occur in more complex \ac{STN} configurations, no existing operator can reduce it to a single equivalent edge. A natural candidate would be to apply $\otimes_{TE}$ iteratively over consecutive referral edges. However, this approach leads to inconsistency with $\otimes_{ME}$. Consider a generic referral path as depicted in \cref{fig:rs2}. When computing the final discounted opinion $\omega^{A_1}_{X}$ using $\otimes_{TE}$ iteratively, the result depends on the grouping order:
\begin{align} \label{eq:TE}
    \omega^{A_1}_{X} &= \omega^{[A_1;\ldots;A_{n-1}]}_{A_{n}}\otimes_{_{ME}} \omega^{A_{n}}_{X} \\
    &=(\omega^{A_1}_{A_2} \otimes_{_{TE}}( \dots \otimes_{_{TE}} (\omega^{A_{n-1}}_{A_{n}} \otimes_{_{TE}} \omega^{A_{n}}_{X}) \notag \\ 
    &\neq((\omega^{A_1}_{A_2} \otimes_{_{TE}} \dots )\otimes_{_{TE}} \omega^{A_{n-1}}_{A_{n})} \otimes_{_{TE}} \omega^{A_{n}}_{X} \, \text{non associativity of } \otimes_{_{TE}} \notag
\end{align}
This non-associativity means that applying $\otimes_{TE}$ over referral-only paths produces results that are inconsistent with $\otimes_{ME}$. To address this issue, we introduce a new \emph{Referral-Edge Path Trust Discounting Operator}, denoted by $\otimes_{RE}$. This operator enables consistent trust propagation over two or more consecutive referral edges while preserving probabilistic coherence implied by the Multi-Edge Trust Discounting.

The dashed green arc shows the intermediate referral result $\omega^{[A_1;\dots;A_{n-1}]}_{A_n}$ obtained by combining all referral edges, while the solid green arc shows the final discounted functional opinion $\omega^{[A_1;\dots;A_n]}_{X}$ about $X$.}

\begin{definition}[Referral-Edges Trust Discounting]
    Let $\omega_B^A = (b_B^A, d_B^A, u_B^A, a_B^A)$ denote the referral trust opinion of agent $A$ about agent $B$, and let $\omega_C^B = (b_C^B, d_C^B, u_C^B, a_C^B)$ denote the referral trust opinion of $B$ about $C$. 

The resulting opinion of $A$ about $C$ through $B$ is defined as:

\begin{align}
\label{equ:tdr_dissertation}
\omega_C^{[A;B]} = \omega_B^A \otimes_{RE} \omega_C^B:
\begin{cases}
b_C^{[A;B]} &= b_B^A \, b_C^B \\
d_C^{[A;B]} &= b_B^A \, d_C^B \\
u_C^{[A;B]} &= 1 - \left( b_C^{[A;B]} + d_C^{[A;B]} \right) \\
a_C^{[A;B]} &= 
\displaystyle
\frac{(b_B^A + u_B^A a_B^A)(b_C^B + u_C^B a_C^B) - b_B^A b_C^B}
{1 - b_B^A (b_C^B + d_C^B)}
\end{cases}
\end{align}

The belief and disbelief are discounted multiplicatively, while the base rate is adjusted to ensure preservation of projected probability.
\end{definition}

A fundamental property of $\otimes_{RE}$ is that it preserves projected probability multiplicativity along referral paths.

\begin{theorem}[Projected Probability Conservation]
\label{thm:projected_probability_preservation}
Let $P_B^A$ and $P_C^B$ denote the projected probabilities of $\omega_B^A$ and $\omega_C^B$ respectively. Then the projected probability of the discounted opinion satisfies:
\begin{equation}
P_C^{[A;B]} = P_B^A \, P_C^B.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By definition of projected probability,
\[
P_C^{[A;B]} = b_C^{[A;B]} + u_C^{[A;B]} a_C^{[A;B]}.
\]
Substituting the expressions from \cref{equ:tdr_dissertation} and simplifying yields:
\[
P_C^{[A;B]} = (b_B^A + u_B^A a_B^A)(b_C^B + u_C^B a_C^B) = P_B^A P_C^B.
\]
\end{proof}
This property guarantees probabilistic consistency across referral chains.

In \ac{DSPG} reduction, referral paths may contain more than two edges. Therefore, the operator must be associative to ensure consistent evaluation of such paths.

\begin{theorem}[Associativity over Multi-Edge Referral Paths]
\label{thm:associativity_RE}
The operator $\otimes_{RE}$ is associative. For any referral chain $A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D$, the following holds:
\begin{equation}
(\omega_B^A \otimes_{RE} \omega_C^B) \otimes_{RE} \omega_D^C
=
\omega_B^A \otimes_{RE} (\omega_C^B \otimes_{RE} \omega_D^C).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The belief component on both sides equals $b_B^A b_C^B b_D^C$, and the disbelief component equals $b_B^A b_C^B d_D^C$. Since projected probability multiplicativity holds recursively, both expressions yield identical projected probabilities. Given equality of belief, disbelief, and projected probability, uncertainty and base rate coincide. Therefore, the two expressions are equal.
\end{proof}

Associativity ensures that trust propagation over referral chains in  \acp{DSPG} is independent of grouping, which is essential for generalization of the operator.

\begin{theorem}[Non Idempotence of the Referral-Edges Trust Discounting]
    The operator $\otimes_{RE}$ is non-idempotent. 
    For an opinion $\omega = (b,d,u,a)$,
    
    \[
    \omega \otimes_{RE} \omega = \omega
    \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
    b = 1 \text{ or } (u = 1 \text{ and } a = 1).
    \]
    
\end{theorem}

This property reflects the fact that repeated referral propagation accumulates uncertainty unless the opinion corresponds to absolute belief or total vacuity.

With averaging fusion, the effect depends on the uncertainty level of $\omega_E^B$. When $u(\omega_E^B)$ is small, retaining the edge reduces the final uncertainty. \paragraph{Summary}

The empirical evaluation confirms three claims of the optimized framework. First, the relaxed criteria of \cref{def:optimized-criteria} translate into measurably higher information retention across all tested graph sizes. Second, the incremental \texttt{nodesToPNPS} update mechanism eliminates the super-linear cost of repeated \ac{NNL} recomputation during synthesis, yielding a several-fold reduction in synthesis time. Third, when executed on the same input \ac{DSPG}, the \ac{PNPS}-based analysis of \cref{fig:algsynth2} is up to $2.2\times$ faster than the classical \ac{PPS}-based analysis, with the gap widening as graph size grows. The slight increase in analysis time (when evaluated after synthesis) observed in \cref{fig:eval_time_breakdown} is therefore not an algorithmic cost but the consequence of retaining more information for trust inference. Taken together with the proofs of \cref{thm:optimized-sound,thm:pnps-reduction}, these suggest that the optimized methodology is consistently preferable to the original one on the graph instances considered: it preserves more information, synthesizes the \ac{DSPG} faster, and analyzes any given \ac{DSPG} faster.

\subsection{Evaluation of Referral-Edge Trust Discounting}
\label{subsec:eval_referral}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{img/discount/eval.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of discounting referral edges using Two-Edge Path operator (TE) and the new operator, Referral-Edge Path (RE) on a transitive graph consisting in a varying number of referral edges with all edges having the same trust opinion: (0.5, 0.4, 0.1, 0.8) as depicted in~\cref{fig:rs2} (subgraph $A_1 \to A_n$). }
    \label{fig:eval}
\end{figure}


In \cref{sec:referral_path_operator}, we analytically proved the equivalence between two methods for computing trust over multi-edge referral paths: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Method 1: Multi-Edge Path discounting.
    \item Method 2: Referral-Edge Path discounting followed by Two-Edge Path discounting.
\end{itemize}
The goal of this section is to numerically validate this equivalence and to illustrate the limitations of the classical Two-Edge Path operator when applied to multiple referral edges. We consider the \ac{STN} depicted in \cref{fig:rs1}. The following experimental setups are evaluated:
\begin{description}
    \item[Setup 1] Computation of $\omega_X^A$ using the Multi-Edge Path operator (Method 1).
    \item[Setup 2] Computation of $\omega_X^A$ using Referral-Edge Path operator followed by Two-Edge Path operator (Method 2).
    \item[Setup 3] Computation of $\omega_X^A$ using the Two-Edge Path operator applied twice.
\end{description}

Using the opinion values from \cref{fig:rs1}, we obtain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Setup 1:
    \[
    \omega_X^A 
    =
    [\omega^A_B; \omega^B_C] \otimes_{ME} \omega^C_X
    =
    (0.14, 0.07, 0.79)
    \]

    \item Setup 2:
    \[
    \omega_X^A
    =
    (\omega_B^A \otimes_{RE} \omega_C^B) \otimes_{TE} \omega_X^C
    =
    (0.14, 0.07, 0.79)
    \]
\end{enumerate}

The identical numerical results confirm the analytical equivalence established earlier. We now apply Setup 3, where the Two-Edge Path operator is used to discount two referral edges:

\[
\omega_X^A
=
(\omega_B^A \otimes_{TE} \omega_C^B) \otimes_{TE} \omega_X^C
=
(0.22, 0.11, 0.67)
\]

This result differs significantly from Setups 1 and 2.  This numerical inconsistency confirms that the Two-Edge Path operator cannot be applied iteratively to referral edges. It lacks associativity and does not preserve projected probability multiplicativity over referral chains. This validates the necessity of the proposed Referral-Edge Path operator.

To further analyze operator behavior, we consider the generic referral path illustrated in \cref{fig:rs2} (path $A_1 \to A_n$). The path contains only referral edges, each assigned the identical opinion:
\(
(0.5, 0.4, 0.1, 0.8)
\).

The number of intermediate nodes varies, allowing us to observe how belief, disbelief, uncertainty, and projected probability evolve as the referral path length increases. We compare the Referral-Edge Path operator (RE) with the Two-Edge Path operator (TE). The expressions are:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:RE_eval}
\omega^{A_1}_{A_n}
&=
((\omega^{A_1}_{A_2} \otimes_{RE} \dots )
\otimes_{RE}
\omega^{A_{n-2}}_{A_{n-1}})
\otimes_{RE}
\omega^{A_{n-1}}_{A_n} \\
\omega^{A_1}_{A_n}
&=
((\omega^{A_1}_{A_2} \otimes_{TE} \dots )
\otimes_{TE}
\omega^{A_{n-2}}_{A_{n-1}})
\otimes_{TE}
\omega^{A_{n-1}}_{A_n}
% \label{eq:TE_eval}
\end{align}

\cref{fig:eval} illustrates the evolution of belief, disbelief, uncertainty, and projected probability as the path length increases. For both operators, Belief decreases, Disbelief decreases and Uncertainty increases. However, their projected probability behaviors differ fundamentally:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For the Referral-Edge operator, projected probability decreases toward zero as the path length increases.
    \item For the Two-Edge operator, projected probability increases toward the base rate value $0.8$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary \& Conclusions}
\label{sec:summary_tnsl}

This chapter addressed the three limitations of \ac{TNA-SL} identified in the introduction: the rigidity of J{\o}sang's original \ac{DSPG} synthesis criteria, the computational inefficiency of the classical \ac{DSPG} analysis procedure, and the absence of a discounting operator capable of handling paths composed entirely of referral edges.

Each limitation was resolved within a unified framework built on the notion of \acf{PNPS}. The \ac{PNPS} construct (\cref{sec:pnps}) refined the classical \ac{PPS} by adding a non-intersection requirement, which yielded a characterization of the \ac{DSPG} property (\cref{prop:dspg}) and an isolation guarantee under reduction (\cref{thm:pnps-reduction}). Building on this foundation, the optimized synthesis criteria (\cref{def:optimized-criteria}) relax the strict \acp{NNL} criteria of J{\o}sang's while preserving \ac{DSPG} correctness (\cref{thm:optimized-sound}), the optimized synthesis algorithm (\cref{alg:dspg-synth}) implements these criteria through a \texttt{nodesToPNPS} mapping that turns each criteria check into a constant-time membership test, and the optimized analysis algorithm (\cref{fig:algsynth2}) replaces global \acp{NL} recomputation by the local update rule of \cref{thm:pnps-reduction}. The Referral-Edge Path operator (\cref{sec:referral_path_operator}) closes the operator-level gap by extending discounting to referral-only paths while preserving projected-probability multiplicativity and associativity.

The empirical evaluation (\cref{sec:eval_tnasl}) confirmed each contribution. The optimized criteria preserve $\approx\!10$ percentage points more edges than the original ones across all tested graph sizes. The \texttt{nodesToPNPS} mapping yields a significant reduction in synthesis time, and on identical input \acp{DSPG} the \ac{PNPS}-based analysis is up to $2.2\times$ faster than the classical \ac{PPS}-based analysis (this gap increases with the size of the graph). The Referral-Edge Path operator was numerically shown to be consistent with the Multi-Edge Path operator, while the iterative application of the Two-Edge Path operator does not.

Taken together, these results establish that the proposed methodology preserves more information than J{\o}sang's original methodology and remains computationally cheaper end-to-end, making it better suited to large and complex networks.
