\section{Role of the General Trust Assessment Methodology}
\label{sec:ch4_role}

This chapter introduces a general trust assessment methodology that serves as the conceptual backbone of this dissertation. The methodology is grounded in the evidence-based trust level evaluation framework for multi-agent systems, where trust is computed through a structured process that includes expression generation, evidence collection, and expression evaluation. In that framework, trust is not assumed or statically assigned. Instead, it is derived from observable evidence and evaluated against predefined requirements.

The goal of this chapter is to abstract and generalize this evidence-based approach so that it can be instantiated across different AI contexts. In later chapters, this methodology will be applied to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the trust assessment of calibration of neural network predictions (\cref{chap:bb}),
    \item the evaluation of training dataset trustworthiness (\cref{chap:data}),
    \item the propagation of trust through neural architectures using the PaTAS framework (\cref{chap:ptas}).
\end{itemize}

Although these application domains differ in their technical details, they
share a common structure. In each case, a trustor seeks to evaluate whether a
specific trust proposition holds for a given entity or process, based on
available evidence and under contextual requirements. The methodology
presented in this chapter formalizes this common structure. More precisely, the methodology provides the different steps one should go through and advice on how to complete each of them. 

By separating trust modeling from domain-specific instantiations, this chapter ensures that the same reasoning principles can be reused across different layers of the AI system, including data, and models. This separation also promotes modularity, and consistency in trust evaluation.

The methodology presented here should therefore be understood as a generic trust reasoning engine. Subsequent chapters will specialize this engine to specific trustworthiness properties, but they will all follow the same conceptual pipeline defined in this chapter.

In summary, this chapter establishes the theoretical and procedural foundation that unifies the contributions of this dissertation. It provides a reusable and domain-independent framework for evidence-based trust quantification in AI
systems.



\section{Core Concepts and Notation}
\label{sec:ch4_core_concepts}

This section introduces the fundamental concepts and notation used throughout
the general trust assessment methodology. The terminology is consistent with
the evidence-based trust level evaluation framework and is designed to remain
applicable across different application domains.

\subsection{Trustor, Trustee, and Trust Proposition}

Trust assessment is inherently relational. A trust evaluation is always
performed from the perspective of a \emph{trustor} regarding a specific
\emph{trustee} and with respect to a well-defined \emph{trust proposition}.

\begin{definition}[Trust Proposition]
A trust proposition is a declarative statement about a property of a trustee
that can be evaluated as satisfied or not satisfied based on evidence.
\end{definition}

Formally, let $A$ denote the trustor and $E$ the trustee. Let $P$ be a
trustworthiness property of interest, such as accuracy, integrity,
representativeness, or calibration reliability. The trust proposition is
denoted by:

\[
\mathcal{T}(E, P)
\]

and represents the statement: ``Entity $E$ satisfies property $P$.''

Trust propositions may be:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simple}, when they refer to a single assessable
    property.
    \item \textbf{Composite}, when they are constructed as logical combinations of simple propositions.
    \item \textbf{Atomic}, proposition that is about a direct relationship in a trust networks.
\end{itemize}

Composite trust propositions are typically expressed using logical operators
such as conjunction, disjunction, and negation. For example, a composite
proposition may take the form:

\[
P = P_1 \wedge P_2
\]

where each $P_i$ is an atomic proposition supported by independent evidence.

\subsection{Actual Trust Level (ATL)}

The outcome of evaluating a trust proposition based on collected evidence is
called the \emph{Actual Trust Level}.

\begin{definition}[Actual Trust Level]
The Actual Trust Level (ATL) is the quantified trust opinion assigned by the
trustor to a trust proposition, based on the available evidence and the
selected evaluation formalism.
\end{definition}

In this dissertation, the ATL is represented as a subjective opinion:

\[
\omega = (b, d, u)
\]

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $b$ denotes belief that the proposition holds,
    \item $d$ denotes disbelief that the proposition holds,
    \item $u$ denotes uncertainty due to insufficient or conflicting evidence,
\end{itemize}
with the constraint $b + d + u = 1$.

The ATL captures not only whether a proposition is supported or contradicted,
but also how confident the trustor can be in this assessment. This explicit
representation of uncertainty distinguishes trust assessment from simple
binary decision mechanisms.

\subsection{Trust Sources and Evidence}

Trust assessment relies on evidence collected from \emph{trust sources}.

\begin{definition}[Trust Source]
A trust source is any entity, mechanism, or process that provides evidence
relevant to the evaluation of a trust proposition.
\end{definition}

Trust sources may include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item direct observations by the trustor,
    \item measurements from sensors or monitoring systems,
    \item reports or certifications from third parties,
    \item historical performance records,
    \item outputs of analytical or verification mechanisms.
\end{itemize}

Evidence may be:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct}, when the trustor observes the trustee directly.
    \item \textbf{Indirect}, when the trustor relies on another entity's
    opinion or report.
\end{itemize}

Indirect evidence introduces \emph{trust relationships} between entities.

\subsection{Trust Relationships}

In multi-agent settings, trust evaluation often depends on referral trust.

\begin{definition}[Trust Relationship]
A trust relationship represents the extent to which a trustor relies on
another entity for forming or updating a trust opinion about a given
proposition.
\end{definition}

If trustor $A$ relies on intermediary $B$ to evaluate proposition $P$, then
$A$ must first assess trust in $B$ regarding $P$. This introduces a
dependency chain that may require discounting or fusion operators during
evaluation.

Trust relationships therefore determine how indirect evidence influences the
final ATL. They also define the structural topology of the trust model, which
will later be formalized through trust expressions.

\section{The Trust Assessment Process}
\label{sec:ch4_three_phase}

Having introduced the core concepts and notation, we now present the
general trust assessment process. This process follows the evidence-based
trust level evaluation methodology and consists of three sequential phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Expression Generation [TLEE]
    \item Atomic Opinion Quantification
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Express the proposition as a logical combination of atomic propositions
        \item Identify trust sources and Evidence Collection for each atomic proposition
        \item Quantify the trust opinion for each trust source using the most approximate quantification method.
        \item Assess the trust in the atomic proposition
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Expression Evaluation [TLEE]
\end{enumerate}

The output of this process is the Actual Trust Level (ATL) associated with
a given trust proposition. Each phase is described in detail below.

\subsection{Phase I: Expression Generation}
\label{subsec:ch4_expression_generation}

The purpose of expression generation is to formally construct a trust
expression that captures all relevant relationships required to evaluate
the trust proposition.

Let $\mathcal{T}_{A}(E, P)$ denote the trust proposition introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:ch4_core_concepts}. In general, $P$ is not directly
measurable. Instead, it must be decomposed into atomic propositions that
are individually assessable.

\subsubsection{Logical Decomposition}

A trust proposition $P$ is decomposed into a set of simple propositions:

\[
P = f(P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n)
\]

where $f(\cdot)$ is a logical composition operator such as conjunction or
disjunction.

For example, a proposition stating that a system operates with integrity
may require that:

\[
P = P_{\text{software integrity}} \wedge
P_{\text{communication integrity}} \wedge
P_{\text{device authenticity}}
\]

Each $P_i$ must correspond to a property for which a subjective trust networks can be derived.

The correctness of this decomposition is critical. If relevant simple propositions are omitted, the final ATL may be wrong. Conversely, excessive decomposition may introduce unnecessary complexity and uncertainty. The level of atomicity must therefore be aligned with
the assessment context.

\subsubsection{Trust Relationships}

In multi-agent environments, atomic propositions may depend on trust
relationships. If trustor $A$ relies on entity $B$ to evaluate $P_i$, then
the trust expression must explicitly incorporate the referral relationship:

\[
\mathcal{T}_{A}(E, P_i)
\quad \text{depends on} \quad
\mathcal{T}_{A}(B, P_i)
\]

Expression generation therefore defines the structural topology of the
trust model. This topology determines how evidence and intermediate
opinions will later be propagated and combined.

At the end of Phase I, the trust proposition is represented as a structured
expression composed of atomic propositions and trust relationships. This
expression serves as the blueprint for the remaining phases.

\subsection{Phase II: Atomic Opinion Quantification}
\label{subsec:ch4_atomic_quantification}

Once the trust expression has been defined in Phase I, the next phase
consists of quantifying trust in that expression. Since composite
expressions are built from atomic propositions, this phase focuses on
quantifying trust at the atomic level.

More precisely, this phase consists of the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Express the proposition as a logical combination of atomic propositions.
    \item Identify trust sources and collect evidence for each atomic proposition.
    \item Quantify the trust opinion for each trust source using the most appropriate quantification method.
    \item Assess the trust in the atomic proposition.
\end{enumerate}

The first two steps establish the structural and evidential foundations of
atomic trust assessment. The remaining steps transform collected evidence
into subjective opinions.

\subsubsection{Identification of Trust Sources}

For each atomic proposition $P_i$, relevant trust sources must be identified.
A trust source refers to any entity, system, or mechanism that provides the
basis for assessing trust.

Trust sources may include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a monitoring component,
    \item a sensor,
    \item a verification module,
    \item a certification authority,
    \item a historical performance record,
    \item another agent providing an opinion.
\end{itemize}

Trust sources may provide direct or indirect evidence. Direct evidence
corresponds to observations made by the trustor itself. Indirect evidence
requires the evaluation of trust in the source before incorporating its
opinion into the final assessment. In such cases, referral trust mechanisms
must be applied during the evaluation phase.

\subsubsection{Evidence Types and Representation}

Evidence collected from trust sources may take different forms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Binary outcomes such as pass or fail,
    \item Counts of successful and unsuccessful events,
    \item Continuous measurements,
    \item Confidence scores,
    \item Reports from external entities.
\end{itemize}

To ensure compatibility with the trust evaluation formalism, evidence must
be mapped into a structured representation. In this dissertation, evidence
is typically converted into:

\begin{itemize}
    \item positive evidence $r_x$,
    \item negative evidence $s_x$,
    \item or structured uncertainty parameters.
\end{itemize}

If evidence is not directly countable, an appropriate interpretation scheme
must be devised to convert raw inputs into usable evidence components.
For instance, binary votes such as approve or disapprove provide directly
countable evidence. In contrast, scalar ratings such as a score on a
1--5 scale require a mapping strategy to distinguish support, opposition,
and uncertainty. Similarly, continuous measurements may require thresholding,
normalization, or probabilistic transformation before being translated into
evidence counts.

\subsubsection{Freshness and Dynamic Context}

Evidence collection is not static. New observations, updated measurements,
or changes in system configuration may alter the available evidence.
Therefore, trust assessment must support dynamic updates.

If new evidence becomes available, the corresponding atomic opinions must
be revised and the entire trust expression re-evaluated. This ensures that
the Actual Trust Level reflects the current system state rather than outdated
information.

\subsubsection{Trust Opinion Quantification}

After evidence has been identified and structured, the next step consists of
quantifying the trust opinion for each trust source.

In general, trust opinion quantification can be performed using different
methods, each reflecting specific assumptions about uncertainty. In addition
to the Baseline-Prior Quantification method presented in
Section~\ref{background} (see Eq.~\ref{eq:q1}), we introduce two additional
methods: Evidence-Weighted Quantification and Constant-Uncertainty
Quantification.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Evidence-Weighted Quantification.}
    This method introduces a weight $w_x$ to account for uncertainty in
    addition to positive and negative evidence. It is particularly relevant
    when the evidence itself is associated with an uncertainty score.
    The belief, disbelief, and uncertainty are computed as:

    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:q3}
    \begin{aligned}
    \begin{cases}
        b_x &= \frac{r_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}, \\
        d_x &= \frac{s_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}, \\
        u_x &= \frac{w_x}{w_x + r_x + s_x}.
    \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Constant-Uncertainty Quantification.}
    Unlike the previous models, this method maintains a fixed uncertainty
    level $U$, regardless of the number of evidence points. This is useful
    when uncertainty is predetermined by factors external to the number of
    positive and negative evidence.

    The belief and disbelief are scaled using a normalization factor $\gamma$:

    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:q2}
    \begin{cases}
        u_x &= U, \\
        \gamma &= \frac{1-U}{r_x + s_x}, \\
        b_x &= \gamma \times r_x, \\
        d_x &= \gamma \times s_x.
    \end{cases}
    \end{align}

\end{enumerate}

Each of these methods provides a distinct approach to handling uncertainty.
The choice of quantification model depends on the characteristics of the
available evidence, the semantics of uncertainty in the application context,
and the desired level of confidence in the trust assessment.

After quantifying the opinions associated with individual trust sources,
the resulting atomic opinions can be aggregated in order to derive the
overall trust in the atomic proposition. This aggregation step is addressed
in Phase III.

\subsection{Phase III: Expression Evaluation}
\label{subsec:ch4_expression_evaluation}

The final phase consists of evaluating the trust expression using the
collected evidence.

\subsubsection{Opinion Quantification}

For each atomic proposition $P_i$, the collected evidence is mapped into
a subjective opinion:

\[
\omega_i = (b_i, d_i, u_i)
\]

The quantification model used for this mapping may vary depending on the
nature of the evidence. For example, baseline-prior quantification,
evidence-weighted quantification, or constant-uncertainty quantification
may be selected depending on whether uncertainty decreases with evidence
volume or remains externally constrained.

\subsubsection{Combination of Atomic Opinions}

Once atomic opinions are derived, they are combined according to the
structure defined in Phase I.

Logical operators such as conjunction and disjunction are implemented
through the corresponding Subjective Logic operators. If referral trust
relationships are present, trust discounting is applied prior to fusion.

If atomic propositions are independent, cumulative fusion may be used.
If evidence sources are correlated, averaging or weighted fusion may be
more appropriate. The selection of fusion operators must be consistent
with the dependency structure defined during expression generation.

\subsubsection{Derivation of the Actual Trust Level}

After recursively evaluating the trust expression, a final subjective
opinion is obtained:

\[
\omega_P = (b_P, d_P, u_P)
\]

This opinion constitutes the Actual Trust Level (ATL) associated with
the original trust proposition $\mathcal{T}_{A}(E, P)$.

The ATL may then be compared with the Required Trust Level (RTL) defined
in Section~\ref{sec:ch4_core_concepts}. If the ATL satisfies the RTL
constraints, the trustee is considered trustworthy in the given context.
Otherwise, corrective actions or mitigation strategies may be required.

\subsection{Summary of the Three-Phase Process}

The three-phase process can therefore be summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Construct a structured trust expression that decomposes the
    proposition into atomic components.
    \item Collect and structure evidence from relevant trust sources.
    \item Quantify and combine subjective opinions to derive the ATL.
\end{enumerate}

This structured pipeline ensures that trust assessment remains transparent,
reproducible, and adaptable across domains. In subsequent chapters, this
generic process will be instantiated for dataset trustworthiness,
calibration reliability, and neural network trust propagation.
